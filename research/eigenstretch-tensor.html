EIGENSTRETCH TENSOR

CAUSAL-TOPOLOGICAL FINGERPRINT OF PARAMETER MANIFOLDS · VERSION 2

A monitor for drift in the sensitivity pattern of a parameter search, recomputed at every checkpoint so I can see when it starts to move. It ships with fixed math, published code, and a measured answer to whether it warns early.

ABSTRACT

I wanted a way to notice when the score surface of a parameter search changes shape while the search is still running. So I took the per-parameter weights from the Reffelt Constant and recomputed them at every checkpoint. That's the Eigenstretch Tensor. When the weights shift between checkpoints, I read that as the score surface changing shape, and I use it as a flag to go look at the run. The name predates the math: what it computes is a vector of per-parameter stretch weights and a digit string, not a tensor.

The other difference from the Reffelt page is the graph. Here it's built from equity-curve similarity, where an equity curve is the running P&L of a strategy. I compare the whole output curve of one configuration with the whole output curve of another using the Wasserstein-1 distance. So two configurations with very different parameter values still sit next to each other if they behave the same.

Measured on simulated searches with a known regime change: the stretch vector moves after the score drops, not before. What it adds is which knob took over, something a score monitor can't tell you. That's its job: a score monitor says something broke, the stretch vector says where.

Status, 2026-10-05. The Laplacian and its eigensolver are standard. The curve graph, the stretch vector and the drift statistic are my construction, now fixed and published as eigenstretch.py (NumPy only; it imports the Reffelt code). Every number below comes from eigenstretch_results.json: simulated searches with a regime change at a known bar, 30 runs per scenario, 40 more stationary runs to set the threshold. When the knob that matters swaps, the stretch vector separates changed runs from unchanged ones better than the score-based baselines (ROC AUC 0.86 against 0.75 for a Page-Hinkley test on live performance). Limits: it fires late, about 500 bars after the score drops; it's noisy at a strict false-alarm budget; and it can't see an optimum that moves while the same knob stays in charge. The old 0.9 and 0.7 thresholds fired on every run, changed or not. All results are synthetic. Not measured yet: a real-data run (the original optimizer logs aren't on this machine).

WHAT CHANGED FROM V1

v1 was a private script that diverged from this page; v2 is the page, in code. What moved:

  • Eigensolver. v1 took the 16 largest singular values of the raw k-NN adjacency matrix. Singular values also pick up strongly negative eigenvalues, whose modes are the opposite of smooth. v2 takes the 16 smallest nonzero eigenpairs of the normalized Laplacian, the same solver as the Reffelt page.
  • Distance. v1 used plain L1 between z-scored curves, unsorted. v2 uses the 1-D Wasserstein-1 distance the page always described: sort each curve's values, then average the absolute differences.
  • Edge weights. v1 left every edge at weight 1. v2 uses E1 below, with score similarity.
  • What gets compared. v1 correlated the eigenvalues with a frozen day-one reference. v2 compares the per-parameter stretch vector, which is the thing that can say which knob changed.
  • Thresholds. The hand-picked 0.9 and 0.7 are gone. The alarm threshold is now calibrated on stationary runs to a 5% false-alarm rate, and the page shows the whole trade-off curve.
  • Digits. Same rule as Reffelt v2: one digit per parameter, 0 below average, 5 to 9 on a doubling scale above it. The 800 scale and the j mod D pairing are gone.

CONSTRUCTION

STEP 1 — ELITE ARCHIVE SELECTION

At each checkpoint, the search re-scores every configuration it has evaluated on the latest window of data and keeps the top N. That keeps the analysis on the part of the space I care about. N = 768 in the benchmark below; the earlier code used 4,096.

STEP 2 — WASSERSTEIN k-NN GRAPH

Each configuration in the archive has an output curve over the window: for a strategy, its equity curve. For any two curves I take the one-dimensional Wasserstein-1 distance. With equal-length curves it's exact and cheap: sort each curve's values and average the absolute differences (it's also the building block of sliced Wasserstein; Rabin, Peyré, Delon & Bernot, SSVM 2011). I divide all distances by their median so ε has no units, then connect each configuration to its 32 nearest curves:

EQUATION E1 — TRAJECTORY SIMILARITY
$$W_1(a, b) = \frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\left|a_{(t)} - b_{(t)}\right|, \qquad w_{ij} = \frac{\exp\left(-\frac{|s_i - s_j|}{\sigma_s}\right)}{W_1(\text{curve}_i, \text{curve}_j)/\operatorname{median}(W_1) + \epsilon}$$

a(t) is the t-th smallest value of curve a, σs is the score standard deviation, ε = 0.1. Why W1? It compares the whole distribution of two curves and doesn't punish a time shift the way L2 on the raw samples does. So two configurations with very different parameters still sit next to each other if they behave the same. That's the point: the graph follows what the configurations do, not where they are.

STEP 3 — LAPLACIAN EIGENDECOMPOSITION

From that graph I form the symmetric normalized Laplacian and keep the 16 smallest nonzero eigenpairs, the smooth end:

EQUATION E2 — SPECTRAL BASIS
$$L_{\text{norm}} = I - D^{-1/2}AD^{-1/2}, \qquad L_{\text{norm}}\psi_j = \lambda_j \psi_j, \qquad 0 < \lambda_1 \le \cdots \le \lambda_{16}$$

STEP 4 — THE STRETCH VECTOR

Now the part that ties the curve graph back to the parameters. This graph isn't built over parameters, so the Reffelt page's winners' reading, which reads the geometry of where winners sit in parameter space, doesn't apply. What does apply is its unbiased reading: smooth the score over the curve graph, then ask how much the smoothed score depends on each parameter, with Chatterjee's ξ along each parameter's sort order:

EQUATION E3 — STRETCH VECTOR
$$\hat s = \sum_{j=1}^{16} c_j D^{-1/2}\psi_j,\quad c_j = \psi_j^{\top}D^{1/2}(s - \bar s_D), \qquad \text{stretch}_d = \frac{\max(\xi(x_d, \hat s), 0)}{\sum_{d'} \max(\xi(x_{d'}, \hat s), 0)}$$

The result has D entries that sum to one. Each entry says how much the score, as the curve graph sees it, depends on that parameter. That vector is the thing I keep and compare between checkpoints! The digit string is the same encoding as Reffelt v2 (R5 there), one digit per parameter.

STEP 5 — DRIFT STATISTIC

I average the stretch vectors of the first four checkpoints into a reference, then at every checkpoint after that compute one minus the correlation with it. I also tried a two-window version that compares the mean of the last three checkpoints with the three before them, so a slow drift doesn't build up against a stale reference:

EQUATION E4 — DRIFT
$$\delta_t = 1 - \operatorname{corr}\left(\text{stretch}_t,\ \tfrac{1}{4}\sum_{u=1}^{4}\text{stretch}_u\right), \qquad \delta^{(2)}_t = 1 - \operatorname{corr}\left(\overline{\text{stretch}}_{t-2..t},\ \overline{\text{stretch}}_{t-5..t-3}\right)$$

An alarm fires the first time the statistic crosses a threshold h. I set h from 40 stationary calibration runs so that 5% of them would have raised an alarm anywhere in the monitored span, then tested on 30 fresh runs per scenario.

WHAT I MEASURED

I needed searches where I know exactly when and how the score surface changes, so I simulated them. A strategy has 10 parameters. Four of them set its exposure to three market factors: one knob sets the exposure to factor 1 (the trend), two knobs together set the exposure to factor 2, and one sets factor 3. The other six do nothing. Each configuration's equity curve is its exposures times the factor returns, plus its own noise. Score is the Sharpe ratio over the last 400 bars.

The search is a small evolution strategy. Every 100 bars it re-scores everything it has seen on the latest window, keeps the top 768 as the archive, computes the stretch vector, and breeds 64 new configurations from the top 32 (plus 16 random ones). 3,000 bars, 27 checkpoints, the first 4 used as the reference. Four scenarios, all with the change at bar 1700:

  • Control. Factor 1 pays the whole time. Nothing changes.
  • Swap. At bar 1700 the payoff jumps from factor 1 to factor 2. The knob that matters changes.
  • Ramp. The same move, spread over 800 bars.
  • Flip. Factor 1 still drives everything, but its sign flips. The best value of the same knob moves to the other end.

Each scenario gets 30 runs on seeds 0 to 29, and a run shares its seed with its control twin, so the two are identical up to bar 1700. 40 more control runs (seeds 10000 to 10039) set the alarm thresholds. The baselines watch the score instead of the weights: a Page-Hinkley test for a drop in the elite's in-sample score, and the same test on live performance, meaning how the top 16 configurations do over the next 100 bars after they're picked. That second one is what I'd watch with money on the line, and it's the right yardstick for “early warning”.

The stretch vector sees the swap. On average it's unmistakable. At bar 1700, the trend knob carries 0.52 of the stretch vector and the two new knobs 0.13. Three hundred bars after the swap it's 0.12 and 0.42, and by bar 2200 the new pair holds 0.55. Over the 1,000 bars after the change, it separates changed runs from their unchanged twins better than every baseline here: ROC AUC 0.86 for the swap and 0.84 for the ramp, against 0.75 and 0.76 for Page-Hinkley on live performance and 0.67 and 0.73 on the in-sample score. The two-window version scores 0.84 and 0.74.

But it's late. The live score drops first: the top configurations' next-100-bar Sharpe falls from 1.02 to 0.24 right after the swap, so on average the drop is visible by bar 1800 (bar 2000 for the ramp). At the calibrated threshold the stretch vector caught 11 of 30 swaps with a median delay of 600 bars (quartiles 350 to 650), every one of them after the drop; median lead −500 bars. On the ramp, 1 of its 8 alarms came before the drop. So this setup gives no early warning. The likely cause: the weights are computed on a 400-bar window, so they can't move until the new regime fills a good part of it, while live performance feels it in the first 100 bars.

And it's noisy at a strict budget. A single checkpoint's stretch vector wobbles a lot, so to keep false alarms near 5% the threshold ends up at a correlation of −0.26 with the reference. That's why only 37% of swaps are caught, while 13% of the test controls still alarm (the calibration set allowed 5%; 30 test runs is a small sample). The two-window version cuts the test-control false alarms to 3%, but catches only 23% of swaps. The score baselines don't do better at this budget: neither Page-Hinkley test caught a single swap or ramp, because their own false alarms from bad-luck stretches set a high bar.

It can't see a flip. When the same knob stays in charge and only its best value moves, the weights barely change: ROC AUC 0.56, close to a coin. Page-Hinkley on live performance gets 0.92 there, and even the old v1 monitor gets 0.77. That's the split in one line: the stretch vector tracks which knob matters, the score tracks whether things got worse, and you want both.

The old numbers. With the hand-picked rules from v1 of this page, a correlation below 0.9 or 0.7 with the reference, every single run alarmed, controls included. The old script's own check (eigenvalue correlation below 0.9) never fired in any run, change or not. Its eigenvalues barely move, and its window ROC AUC on the swap is 0.59.

OVERFITTING

Digits climbing shortly before the out-of-sample score drops. Not tested: my simulation has regime changes, not overfitting. And in the regime changes I did test, the weights moved after the score, not before.

REGIME CHANGE

Measured. When the knob that matters swaps, the stretch vector shows it clearly on average and points at the new knobs, but late and noisily per run. When only the optimum moves, it doesn't see it.

DIMENSIONAL COLLAPSE

Seen in every control run: as the search converges, weight drains from the knob the archive has settled on. During a search that's convergence, not a problem, but it drifts any frozen reference. On a deployed model it's untested.

REFERENCE CORRELATION

The 0.9 and 0.7 rules fired on every run. A threshold calibrated on stationary runs replaces them, and the ROC chart above shows the trade-off at every other threshold.

LIMITS

  • Everything is simulated, with one market model, one search, 10 parameters and 768-configuration archives. No real-data number yet: the BTC, ETH and SOL optimizer runs aren't on this machine.
  • The lead-time result depends on the 400-bar window and the 100-bar checkpoint spacing. A shorter window might move the weights sooner; the sweep is next.
  • 30 test runs per scenario is enough for the big differences on this page and not for small ones. The 13% control false-alarm rate against a 5% target is the kind of gap that sample size allows.
  • ADWIN isn't in the comparison, and the Page-Hinkley tests use the plain running mean with no tolerance term. A tuned score monitor would likely do better.
  • The calibrated threshold is specific to this simulation. On a new system you'd need your own stationary runs, or a stretch of history you trust, to set it.

HOW TO REPRODUCE

Grab eigenstretch.py and reffelt_constant.py into one folder. NumPy is the only dependency.

COMMANDS
python3 eigenstretch.py bench --seeds 30 --cal 40 --jobs 10   # writes ../eigenstretch_results.json, about 40 minutes on 10 cores
python3 eigenstretch.py check                                  # recomputes every metric from the per-run traces in the JSON
python3 eigenstretch.py demo --kind swap --seed 0              # one run, digits and drift at each checkpoint

Everything is seeded, so a rerun gives the same JSON apart from the date and the runtime. The JSON keeps the drift statistics, live scores and stretch shares of all 160 runs, so you can check my evaluation without rerunning the simulation. The older private script is retired; its monitor is reproduced inside the benchmark as the v1 detector.

RELATED WORK

The job this page does, flagging a change in a stream of checkpoints, has established tools. The Page-Hinkley test (Page, Biometrika 41, 1954) is a cumulative-sum test for a shift in the mean, and I ran it on two score streams as the baseline. ADWIN (Bifet & Gavaldà, SDM 2007) keeps an adaptive window and flags when its two halves disagree; my two-window statistic is a simple version of that idea, and ADWIN itself isn't run here. For overfitting specifically, Prechelt's early-stopping criteria (“Early Stopping, But When?”, 1998) are the standard baseline, still untested here. The stretch vector itself rests on Chatterjee's rank correlation (J. Amer. Statist. Assoc. 116, 2021) and on the graph-Laplacian machinery cited on the Reffelt page.

THINGS I'D LIKE TO TRY

Where I want to take it next. None of these have been run.

PAIR IT WITH A SCORE MONITOR

The results say the two signals are complementary: the score says something broke, the stretch vector says which knob took over. The obvious next test is a combined rule, alarm on the score, then read the weights, and whether that re-targets the search faster than restarting it.

SHORTER WINDOWS

If the lag comes from the 400-bar window, a shorter one should cut it, at the price of noisier weights. The next measurement is lead time against window length, which settles whether early warning is reachable at all.

TRAINING RUNS

Compute the stretch vector at checkpoints across a set of training runs with varied hyperparameters. Does it shift when a learning-rate problem starts? Untested.

DRIFT ON A DEPLOYED MODEL

Compare the current stretch vector with the one from training time and flag divergence. Not yet compared with watching prediction confidence, and given the lag above, expect it to be slow.
