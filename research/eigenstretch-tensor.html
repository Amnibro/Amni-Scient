EIGENSTRETCH TENSOR

CAUSAL-TOPOLOGICAL FINGERPRINT OF PARAMETER MANIFOLDS

A monitor for drift in the sensitivity pattern of a parameter search, recomputed at every checkpoint so I can see when it starts to move.

ABSTRACT

I wanted a way to notice when the score surface of a parameter search changes shape while the search is still running. So I took the Reffelt Constant, the single-snapshot digit string from its own page, and recomputed it at every checkpoint. That's the Eigenstretch Tensor. When the digit pattern shifts between checkpoints, I read that as the score surface changing shape, and I use it as a flag to go look at the run. Quick honesty note on the name: it's older than the math. What I actually compute is a vector of per-dimension stretch weights and a digit string, not a tensor.

The other difference from the Reffelt page is the graph. Here it's built from equity-curve similarity, where an equity curve is the running P&L of a strategy. I compare the whole output curve of one configuration to the whole output curve of another with an approximate Wasserstein-1 distance. So two configurations with very different parameter values still sit next to each other if they behave the same. That's the whole point: I want the graph to follow what the configurations do, not where they are.

Status, 2026-09-09. Here's where this stands before you dig in. The Laplacian and its eigendecomposition are standard, and I cite them below. The trajectory graph, the stretch weights and the digit encoding are my construction, and the 800 scale, the mode-to-dimension pairing and the 0.9 and 0.7 correlation thresholds you'll meet below are all hand-picked. Both charts on this page draw hand-set numbers so you can see the shapes; neither one runs a decomposition. There's an older implementation on disk, unpublished, and it doesn't match this page yet; How to Reproduce has the differences. And the lead time between a digit spike and a score drop? I think I saw it in my old optimizer runs, but I kept no logs, so it's an anecdote and there's no measured number on this page.

CONSTRUCTION

STEP 1 — ELITE ARCHIVE SELECTION

From the population of evaluated configurations I keep the top N by fitness score. That keeps the spectral analysis on the region of parameter space I care about: the neighborhood of the best configurations. N=4096 is what my unpublished implementation uses. Nothing is published yet, so treat every constant on this page as a design choice.

STEP 2 — WASSERSTEIN k-NN GRAPH

Next I build a sparse k-nearest-neighbor graph over the archive with k=64 (a design choice), using an approximate Wasserstein-1 distance between output curves. For W1 I use the one-dimensional form: sort the values of each curve, then take the L1 distance between the two sorted cumulative curves. That's exact for 1-D distributions, and it's the building block of sliced Wasserstein (Rabin, Peyré, Delon & Bernot, SSVM 2011). My current code cuts a corner here: it takes plain L1 between the cumulative curves, unsorted, and leaves every edge at weight 1. On this page, edge weights also carry score similarity:

EQUATION E1 — TRAJECTORY SIMILARITY
$$w_{ij} = \frac{\exp\left(-\frac{|s_i - s_j|}{\sigma_s}\right)}{W_1(\text{traj}_i, \text{traj}_j) + \epsilon}$$

σs is the standard deviation of the scores across the archive, and ε is a small constant I would set to keep the denominator away from zero. Why W1? It compares the whole shape of two curves. Plain L2 on the samples also sees every point, but it punishes a time shift hard. W1 doesn't.

STEP 3 — LAPLACIAN EIGENDECOMPOSITION

From that graph I form the symmetric normalized Laplacian and take a rank-16 basis, where 16 is a design choice. The modes I want are the ones with the smallest nonzero eigenvalues, because those vary slowly across the graph.

EQUATION E2 — SPECTRAL BASIS EXTRACTION
$$L_{\text{norm}} = I - D^{-1/2}AD^{-1/2}, \qquad L_{\text{norm}}\psi_j = \lambda_j \psi_j, \qquad j = 1 \ldots 16$$

Here's the snag. My current code doesn't do this step as written. It runs a truncated SVD (scipy's svds with the LOBPCG solver; the code comments call it a randomized SVD) on the raw k-NN adjacency matrix, not the Laplacian, and keeps the 16 largest singular values. On a near-regular graph the top of the adjacency spectrum lines up with the smooth end of the Laplacian, but singular values also pick up strongly negative eigenvalues, and those modes are the opposite of smooth. That's a known inconsistency I still have to fix. The right call is a shift-invert or a partial eigensolver on the small end of the Laplacian.

STEP 4 — PER-DIMENSION GRADIENT SENSITIVITY

Now the part that ties the modes back to the parameters. For each parameter dimension d I sort the archive by that coordinate, take the finite difference of each eigenvector's entries between consecutive configurations in that order, and average the absolute value over the archive. πd is the sorting permutation:

EQUATION E3 — CAUSAL CONTRIBUTION VECTOR
$$g_{j,d} = \frac{1}{N-1}\sum_{i=1}^{N-1}\left|\psi_j(\pi_d(i+1)) - \psi_j(\pi_d(i))\right|, \qquad \text{causal}_d = \frac{\sum_j g_{j,d}}{\sum_{d'} \sum_j g_{j,d'}}$$

The result is a vector with D entries that sum to one. Each entry says how much the smooth modes stretch along that parameter axis. That vector is the thing I keep and compare between checkpoints!

STEP 5 — DIGIT ENCODING

Last, I squash it into digits so I can eyeball it. Digit j is eigenvalue j times a causal weight, scaled by 800, plus one, clamped to 1 through 8. A product over 1.0 prints as 9 so I can spot saturation, and a product under 0.01 prints as 0. Two of those choices are guesses. Right now I pair mode j with dimension j mod D, which is a placeholder; the pairing isn't justified yet. And the 800 is a hand-picked scale, same as on the Reffelt page, with the same flaw: for any product of 0.01 or more, 800 × product + 1 is already 9 or more, so every digit between the two flags prints as 8. The code doesn't use this rule at all yet; How to Reproduce says what it does instead.

EQUATION E4 — EIGENSTRETCH DIGITS
$$d_j = \operatorname{clamp}\left(\left\lfloor 800 \cdot \lambda_j \cdot \text{causal}_{j \bmod D} \right\rfloor + 1,\; 1,\; 8\right), \qquad d_j = 9 \text{ if } \lambda_j \text{causal}_{j \bmod D} > 1.0, \qquad d_j = 0 \text{ if } \lambda_j \text{causal}_{j \bmod D} < 0.01$$

The output is a 16-digit string using digits 0 to 9, with 0 and 9 reserved as flags.

DIAGNOSTIC SIGNALS

So what do I actually look for? These are the four conditions I check at each checkpoint. Treat them as a checklist I wrote for myself, not as validated detectors.

OVERFITTING

This is what the monitor is meant to catch: digits climbing toward 8 and 9 shortly before the out-of-sample score drops. I think I saw that in my old optimizer runs, but I kept no logs, so it's an anecdote, and I'm not quoting a lead time.

REGIME CHANGE

If the digit pattern changes a lot between checkpoints, say from "84417588" to "35621844", I read that as the score surface having changed shape and the old best configurations no longer sitting in the same basin. I don't yet have a threshold for how much change counts, because I haven't measured how much the digits wander on their own.

DIMENSIONAL COLLAPSE

If several digits drop to 1 at once, the smooth modes have stopped moving along those dimensions. During a search, that's what convergence looks like. After a search, on a deployed model, it would mean the model is losing sensitivity. I haven't tested that case.

REFERENCE CORRELATION

I freeze a reference stretch vector at the start and take the correlation between it and the current one at each checkpoint. Above 0.9, I do nothing. Below 0.7, I recompute the reference and look at the run. Both numbers are starting values I picked, not tuned.

RELATED WORK

The job this page is trying to do, flagging a change in a stream of checkpoints, already has established tools. ADWIN (Bifet & Gavaldà, SDM 2007) keeps an adaptive window and flags when the two halves disagree. The Page-Hinkley test is a cumulative-sum test for a shift in the mean. For the overfitting case specifically, Prechelt's early-stopping criteria (“Early Stopping, But When?”, 1998) are the standard baseline. I haven't run any of them against the digits yet, and until I do, I can't say the digits add anything.

THINGS I'D LIKE TO TRY

This is where I want to take it next. Fair warning: none of these have been run. I've used the digits on my own optimizer runs and nowhere else.

TRAINING STABILITY

I'd like to compute the stretch vector at checkpoint intervals during a training run. Does a digit spike line up with a learning-rate problem or with the start of overfitting? Does a digit collapse line up with mode collapse in a generative model? If it does, it could feed an early-stopping rule. Untested.

HYPERPARAMETER SWEEPS

Across a set of training runs with varied hyperparameters, the stretch vector should say which settings the quality depends on. One detail matters here: the digit string has 16 digits, one per mode, while the stretch vector has one entry per hyperparameter. So it's the vector, not the string, that would tell a team what to freeze. I haven't run this on anyone's sweep.

DRIFT ON A DEPLOYED MODEL

Same drift monitor, after the search is over. On a deployed model, the idea would be to compare the current stretch vector against the one from training time and flag divergence. I haven't tried it on a deployed model, and I don't know how it compares with watching prediction confidence.

HARDWARE STRESS ENVELOPES

Run parameterized stress tests over clock, thermal, memory bandwidth and cache settings, compute the stretch vector over the results, and see whether the high-weight axes match the axes the hardware is actually bottlenecked on. Untested idea.

HOW TO REPRODUCE

Bad news first: nothing is published. There is an older implementation on disk, holographic_stretch.py in a private repo, and it isn't this page's pipeline yet. It keeps the top 4,096 configurations, builds a 64-NN graph from plain L1 between equity curves with unit edge weights, takes a truncated SVD of that adjacency matrix (16 largest singular values), projects finite-difference backtest gradients onto each mode (proxy gradients when it has no price data), and prints ⌊8 × value / frozen reference max⌋ + 1 clipped to 1 through 9, with one check: the eigenvalues' correlation with a frozen reference, flagged below 0.9. I have no saved runs of it. So every number on this page is a design choice until a linked script says otherwise, and the two charts above are the only runnable pieces here, both drawing hand-set values. Good news: you can rebuild the pipeline from the text. Keep the top N configurations by score, compute the 1-D W1 distance between every pair of output curves, build the k-NN graph with the E1 weights, form the symmetric normalized Laplacian, take the 16 smallest nonzero eigenpairs, compute the sorted finite-difference weights from E3, and encode with E4. Then repeat at each checkpoint and compare the stretch vectors. Just know that the 800 scale, the j mod D pairing, and the 0.9 and 0.7 thresholds are the places where you'd be copying my guesses.
