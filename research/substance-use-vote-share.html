I joined SAMHSA’s 2021–2023 NSDUH substate small-area estimates to 2024 presidential votes reallocated to ZCTAs by the land-cover method (Harvard Dataverse 10.7910/DVN/Z8TSH3), with ACS 2019–2023 age and education and Census 2020 ZCTA internal points. The unit is the substate region. N = 382 regions, 30,389 ZCTAs, 150.1 million allocated two-party votes. Weights are two-party votes. Standard errors are HC1. The specified model is the rate, log population density, percent ages 18–25, and percent ages 12–17. Ages 12–14 are three-fifths of the ACS 10–14 band, and age 25 is one-fifth of the 25–29 band. Replacing those with ages 15–17 and 18–24 leaves the marijuana slope at 1.12.

Alaska and Hawaii have no rows in the vote file. The shapefile has 393 regions. Nebraska is in it: five regions, FIPS 31, polygons between 104°W and 95°W. The file’s state name on those rows says New Jersey. One New Jersey region, Southern, FIPS 34, is labeled New York. State indicators use the FIPS code. The estimation sample is 49 jurisdictions: 48 states plus the District of Columbia.

The NSDUH rates are small-area estimates. I applied no errors-in-variables correction. No public file links one person’s use to that person’s ballot. Density’s correlation with Democratic two-party share is 0.73. Bachelor’s share is 0.71. The slope that remains after density and age is the residual association of the rate.

Past-month cocaine, heroin, methamphetamine, hallucinogens, opioid misuse, and pain-reliever misuse are not in the substate file. Those six are past-year rates, and heroin is ages 18 or older. There is no separate heavy-drinking field. Binge alcohol in the past month is the intensity measure SAMHSA published. Fentanyl, inhalants, LSD, PCP, and crack are not separate substate rates. The file also carries mental-illness, suicide, and perceived-risk outcomes. I left those out of the vote regression. Substance-use disorder rates are in the second table. They are disorder prevalence, not use.

The user and non-user shares are a vote-weighted regression of Democratic two-party share on the use rate, log density, and age, with density and age held at their vote-weighted means. The sampling distribution of the two shares is restricted to 0–100. A rate whose Duncan–Davis bounds on the user share run from 0 to 100, and whose restricted estimate piles on that edge, stays off that chart. The companion reads the same regression at the 10th and 90th percentiles of the observed regional rate. Democratic-majority regions are those with Democratic two-party share above 50%. The ratio is the vote-weighted use rate there divided by the rate where Republicans took the majority.

The laboratory chart is laboratory data. The potency curves are predictions. Federal contribution totals are OpenSecrets industry codes N02, H04, and N09. The 2024 cycle is January 1, 2023 through December 31, 2024. I rechecked the live N09 page on 2026-10-11. It shows $969,606. OpenSecrets dates that cycle’s FEC release February 6, 2025. Party percentages are the published shares of contributions to candidates and parties. Outside spending is in the total and out of the party split. Federal lobbying is OpenSecrets’ calendar-2024 total from Senate Office of Public Records filings, January through December. State ballot figures are committee totals compiled by Ballotpedia from state filings, retrieved 2026-10-11. Ballot measures have no party. FEC employer and PAC totals for named cannabis companies sit beside the industry total. They are separate filings.

Rate of change

SAMHSA withdrew the 2018–2020 substate estimates. Web interviews in late 2020 changed answers relative to the in-person interviews of 2018 and 2019, and the agency took that three-year pool down. The 2016–2018 substate shapefile is still published. The 2021 methodology guide sets 2021 as the baseline for NSDUH estimates after the move to web and in-person collection. States also redraw substate regions between cycles. The 2021–2023 file is one pooled period. The line on each panel is the rate of change in this cross-section: points of 2024 Democratic two-party share per point of the 2021–2023 rate.

Perception and the vote slope

Group A’s common slope is +0.76 points of Democratic two-party share per point of the rate (clustered SE 0.08). Group B’s is −2.04 (SE 1.03). The difference, group B minus group A, is −2.80 (SE 1.04).

Putting heroin in group B leaves group A at +0.75 (SE 0.08) and moves group B to −1.65 (SE 1.02). That difference is −2.39 (SE 1.03). Heroin on its own, with the same density and age controls, is +8.52 per point (HC1 SE 3.48). Its correlation with log density is +0.24.

Group A is marijuana, hallucinogens, alcohol, and cocaine. Marijuana: Kloft and colleagues vaporized 300 μg of THC per kilogram, and a suggested false detail was accepted 19% of the time versus 8% on placebo. Hallucinogens are serotonin 5-HT2A agonists that alter perception (Nichols, Pharmacological Reviews, 2016, 10.1124/pr.115.011478). LSD raised Creative Imagination Scale ratings versus placebo (Carhart-Harris and colleagues, Psychopharmacology, 2015, 10.1007/s00213-014-3714-z). Alcohol: people told the drink was vodka were more swayed by misleading information (Assefi and Garry, 2003), and severely intoxicated participants went along with suggestive cues more than sober participants (van Oorsouw, Merckelbach, and Smeets, 2015). Cocaine blocks the dopamine transporter. At 0.3–0.6 mg/kg intravenous, 60–77% of transporter sites were occupied and the reported high tracked that occupancy (Volkow and colleagues, Nature, 1997, 10.1038/386827a0).

Group B is opioid misuse, pain-reliever misuse, and methamphetamine. Opioid misuse and pain-reliever misuse are mu-opioid agonists. Agonists at that receptor produce analgesia and sedation (Pathan and Williams, British Journal of Pain, 2012, 10.1177/2049463712438493). Methamphetamine is a monoamine releaser. At 5–30 mg the acute effects are arousal, positive mood, and cardiac stimulation (Cruickshank and Dyer, Addiction, 2009, 10.1111/j.1360-0443.2009.02564.x). It sits in group B because that profile is stimulant arousal. Heroin is a mu-opioid agonist in the same class, and the two pooled fits report it both ways.

Children in homes where parents use

In the ABCD baseline, children whose mothers reported cannabis use after learning they were pregnant scored 81.7 on the NIH Toolbox cognition composite (SD 8.6). Children with no prenatal cannabis exposure scored 86.5 (SD 9.1). The unadjusted standardized difference is −0.061. With the published covariates, that contrast is −0.011 (interval on the unstandardized composite, −2.3 to 0.65). The covariate-adjusted contrast that stays negative is after knowledge versus only before knowledge, −0.023.

Binge prenatal alcohol, pooled across 8 studies and about 10,000 children, is Cohen’s d −0.13 (interval −0.21 to −0.05). Mild-to-moderate prenatal alcohol in the same meta-analysis is d +0.04 (0.00 to 0.08). In Belarus, weekly moderate paternal drinking lines up with −1.6 full-scale IQ points (interval −2.5 to −0.9), and weekly heavy drinking with −2.5 (−3.4 to −1.6). In the Indian case-control of 32 children, full-scale means are 96.14 in the alcohol-dependent-father group and 105.1 in the comparison group.

Laboratory results

Kloft and colleagues (PNAS, 2020) vaporized 300 μg of THC per kilogram. On the immediate test, a suggested false detail in the eyewitness scene was accepted 19% of the time after THC (SE 0.04) and 8% after placebo (SE 0.02). Related-word false alarms were 42% (SE 0.03) and 27% (SE 0.03). Gawrylowicz and colleagues (Psychopharmacology, 2017) gave alcohol in the lab after a filmed crime and before the misleading narrative. The marginal mean proportion of misled responses was 0.25 after alcohol (95% CI 0.196 to 0.300), 0.22 in the reverse-placebo arm (0.174 to 0.276), and 0.319 with no alcohol (0.266 to 0.371).

Suggestibility, predicted from the lab dose

Accepting a detail that was planted, and reporting it as remembered, is the lab measure of suggestibility. That is the recorded piece of what people mean by being led into a false account. Kloft and colleagues (PNAS, 2020) vaporized 300 μg of THC per kilogram, about 21 mg at 70 kg. While still intoxicated, volunteers accepted a suggested false detail in an eyewitness scene 19% of the time (SE 0.04). On placebo the same people accepted it 8% of the time (SE 0.02). Cohen’s d for that contrast is 0.62. Related-word false alarms in the same session moved from 27% to 42% (d = 0.58).

The x-axis is flower potency. The lab dose is placed at 14% THC: half a gram of flower at that potency, with 30% of the THC inhaled, delivers 21 mg. That is an ordinary modern session, not a 1995 joint. The curve is linear on the logit between the placebo and that dose. Past the lab dose, the band adds a further 35% uncertainty on the extra climb, because nobody has rerun this test at concentrate strength. Dots are the measured means. Whiskers are their published standard errors, times 1.96.

IQ, predicted from study potency

Meier and colleagues (PNAS, 2012) followed the Dunedin birth cohort. The most persistent adolescent-onset cannabis users lost 8 IQ points from childhood to age 38. The broader persistent-dependence group lost about 6. The study did not assay THC. Those people were using between the late 1980s and 2010. DEA-seized flower over 1995–2010 averaged 7.1% THC. That is the study rate used here.

The prediction multiplies the 8-point loss by the ratio of potency to 7.1%. Sampling error on the anchor is taken as 2.5 points, which is a within-group SD of about 12 IQ points divided by the square root of 23, the size of that adolescent-onset group. Past 7.1%, the band adds a 50% uncertainty on the extra loss, because the cohort never compared potencies. The dot is the measured 8. The rest of the curve is the scale-up.

Published studies

Kloft, Otgaar, Blokland, Monds, Toennes, Loftus, and Ramaekers, PNAS 2020, 10.1073/pnas.1920162117. Double-blind, placebo-controlled, 64 volunteers. Immediate eyewitness suggested-detail acceptance, 19% on THC and 8% on placebo.

Meier, Caspi, Ambler, Harrington, Houts, Keefe, McDonald, Ward, Poulton, and Moffitt, PNAS 2012, 10.1073/pnas.1206820109. Dunedin birth cohort. Adolescent-onset persistent cannabis users lost 8 IQ points from childhood to age 38.

Topiwala, Allan, Valkanova, and colleagues, BMJ 2017, 10.1136/bmj.j2353. Whitehall II imaging. More than 30 units of alcohol a week, odds ratio 5.8 for hippocampal atrophy versus abstainers (95% CI 1.8 to 18.6). Fourteen to 21 units a week, odds ratio 3.4 for right hippocampal atrophy (1.4 to 8.1). Lexical fluency declined faster above 7 units a week.

Gawrylowicz, Ridley, Albery, Barnoth, and Young, Psychopharmacology 2017, 10.1007/s00213-017-4564-2. Alcohol after the witnessed event, before the misinformation. The no-alcohol arm reported more misled responses than the alcohol and reverse-placebo arms.

Assefi and Garry, Psychological Science 2003, 10.1111/1467-9280.01422. Everyone drank tonic. People told the drink was vodka were more swayed by misleading postevent information than people told it was tonic, F(1, 115) = 4.20.

Van Oorsouw, Merckelbach, and Smeets, Applied Cognitive Psychology 2015, 10.1002/acp.3129. Field study in bars, not a placebo arm. Mean BAC about 0.01%, 0.06%, and 0.16%. Severely intoxicated participants went along with suggestive cues more than sober participants.

Flowe, Humphries, Takarangi, Zelek, Karoğlu, Gabbert, and Hope, Applied Cognitive Psychology 2019, 10.1002/acp.3531. Alcohol versus tonic, crossed with what people were told they drank. People told they had alcohol reported fewer correct details. The paper reports they were no more likely to report misleading information.

Denham, International Journal of Drug Policy 2019, 10.1016/j.drugpo.2019.06.007. General Social Survey, 1986–2016. Party affiliation became a consistent predictor of support for marijuana legalization after 2004. That paper is about legalization attitudes.
