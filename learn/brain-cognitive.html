WHAT THIS PACK INCLUDES

Cognitive-test games for adults built on the standard psychometric paradigms used in working-memory research, attention assessment, and executive-function testing: card-pair concentration, digit-span (Number Memory), N-back, Chimp Test (visual working memory), simple reaction time, mental arithmetic blitz, and the Stroop interference task.

WHAT KIDS PRACTICE

Each game uses scoring conventions that let users track personal progress: digit span by maximum length recalled, N-back by hit rate at each level, reaction time by ms median, Stroop by congruent-vs-incongruent latency. The Chimp Test reproduces the Inoue & Matsuzawa (2007) protocol where digits flash briefly then disappear; users tap them in numerical order. Speed Math escalates difficulty until the user can no longer keep pace, providing a self-calibrating challenge.

The Research Behind These Tasks

Each game in this category reproduces a paradigm with substantial published research, summarized below:

Number Memory (digit span) is a core component of the Wechsler intelligence scales. Forward digit span averages 7 ± 2 items for adults (Miller, 1956); backward span averages 5 ± 1. Span varies with attention, fatigue, and bilingualism (slightly higher in monolingual speakers due to language-specific encoding).

N-Back was introduced by Kirchner (1958) and remains the most-used working-memory paradigm in cognitive neuroscience. Untrained adults reliably perform 2-back; 3-back is challenging; 4-back is rare without practice. Whether N-back training transfers to general intelligence remains debated (Jaeggi et al. 2008 reported transfer; Redick et al. 2013 failed to replicate).

Chimp Test reproduces the Inoue & Matsuzawa (2007) paradigm where chimpanzees outperformed humans on rapid visual digit ordering. Humans typically peak at ~9 digits with 200–500 ms display; Ayumu the chimpanzee performed at 9 digits with 60 ms display.

Stroop Test (Stroop, 1935) measures executive function via the latency difference between congruent (word RED in red ink) and incongruent (RED in blue ink) trials. The interference effect is robust across populations and a sensitive marker of frontal-lobe function in clinical settings.

Things To Know About Self-Testing

  • Practice effects are largeMost cognitive tests improve 10–30% with practice in the first 5–10 attempts, even with no intervention. A single high score isn't baseline; track moving averages over weeks.
  • Time of day mattersMorning peak hours produce 5–15% better scores than late afternoon for most adults. Standardize testing time if you want comparable scores across sessions.
  • Caffeine and sleep are confoundsAcute caffeine improves reaction time and Stroop performance by 5–10%. Sleep deprivation degrades scores 15–30%. Note conditions if recording scores formally.
  • Not a clinical assessmentThese tasks are simplified versions of clinical instruments. They aren't calibrated against norms, don't have inter-rater reliability data, and shouldn't be used to self-diagnose anything. Use them for personal interest and trend tracking, not diagnosis.

User FAQ

Will N-Back training make me smarter?
Strongly contested. The Jaeggi et al. (2008) finding of fluid-intelligence transfer wasn't replicated in larger studies (Redick et al. 2013, Melby-Lervåg & Hulme 2013 meta-analysis). What is consistent: practice improves N-back performance specifically (near transfer), but generalization to other tasks (far transfer) is uncertain. Treat as exercise of attention, not IQ boost.
What's a normal digit span?
7 ± 2 forward, 5 ± 1 backward for adults. Children under 5 are typically ~4 forward. Older adults (65+) decline slightly to 6 ± 2 forward. Spans below 3 forward in adults warrant clinical evaluation; spans above 10 are exceptional and worth verifying with a different method.
Why is my reaction time so much slower than "average"?
Common causes: input latency from the device (monitors, touchscreens add 20–100 ms), age (10 ms slower per decade past 20), screen distance, lighting, and acute factors (fatigue, illness, medications). Median web-browser reaction time is ~270–350 ms; below 200 ms is suspicious of cheating or hardware artifacts.
