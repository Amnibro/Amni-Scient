WHAT THIS PACK INCLUDES

Visual-attention and spatial-reasoning games drawn from neuropsychological assessment paradigms: multi-object tracking, peripheral-flash detection, conjunction visual search, mental rotation (Shepard-Metzler style), and the Trail Making Test parts A and B used clinically for executive-function screening.

WHAT PLAYERS BUILD HERE

Multi-object Dot Tracking presents N targets among distractors, all moving; users mark the targets after movement stops. Peripheral Vision flashes a stimulus briefly at varying eccentricity from a central fixation. Visual Scan demands a target-among-distractors decision under time pressure. Mental Rotation shows two 3D figures and asks "same or mirror." Trail Making asks users to connect numbered (Part A) or alternating number-letter (Part B) targets in order, with timing as the score.

Clinical Roots Of These Tests

Several games in this category come directly from neuropsychological assessment:

Trail Making Test (Reitan, 1958) measures attention-switching and executive function. Part A asks subjects to connect numbers 1-25 in order; Part B alternates numbers and letters (1-A-2-B-...). The B/A time ratio is the standard executive-function metric in clinical batteries (Halstead-Reitan, Cambridge Neuropsychological Test Automated Battery).

Mental Rotation derives from Shepard & Metzler (1971), the foundational study that demonstrated humans rotate mental images at a measurable rate (~60°/second). Reaction time scales linearly with rotation angle, providing direct evidence of analog mental imagery.

Multi-Object Tracking (Pylyshyn & Storm, 1988) measures attentional capacity. Adults typically track 3–5 targets simultaneously; older adults decline to 2–3; expert video-gamers reach 6–8.

Useful field of view (UFOV) tasks like Peripheral Vision are used in elderly driver assessment and military pilot screening. Performance declines with age and is sensitive to early dementia.

What To Watch Out For

  • Mental rotation gender effect is real but smallOlder studies reported large male advantage on mental rotation. Modern meta-analyses (Voyer et al. 1995, Levine et al. 2016) confirm a small effect (d ~ 0.5) attributable partly to practice differences (spatial-game exposure) rather than fundamental difference. Individual variation dwarfs group differences.
  • Trail Making mouse vs touchscreenMouse-and-keyboard interfaces produce slightly slower trail times than touchscreens for the same task (~10–15% slower). For longitudinal tracking, use the same input device every session.
  • Display size affects resultsPeripheral vision tasks calibrated for 24" monitors at 60 cm viewing distance produce different scores on phones held at 30 cm. Larger angular eccentricity = harder task. Note your screen and distance if comparing.
  • Practice effect plateauThese tasks improve rapidly in the first 5–10 sessions, then plateau. Continuing past the plateau provides little measurable benefit; rotating among the games and to brain-cognitive helps maintain engagement.

User FAQ

Can I improve my peripheral vision through training?
Limited evidence. The Useful Field of View (UFOV) training program shows modest improvements (10–30%) that may transfer to driving safety in elderly populations. The peripheral-vision game here is similar but not identical to UFOV; treat improvements as task-specific until evidence shows otherwise.
What is a normal Trail Making B time?
Adults 20–40: 60–75 seconds. Adults 40–60: 75–100 seconds. Adults 60+: 100–150 seconds typical. Clinical concern when B/A ratio exceeds 3.0 or absolute B time exceeds 273 seconds (typical clinical cutoff).
Should mental rotation be hard or easy?
Untrained adults solve 60°–120° rotations in 2–5 seconds. 180° rotations take longer (more cognitive distance). Training reduces times significantly. If 30° rotations take you >5 seconds consistently, eye-tracking studies suggest you may be solving by feature-matching rather than holistic rotation — both are valid strategies.
