LIFE SKILLS

WHAT YOU WILL FIND HERE

Life-skills mini-games teach the practical numeracy and procedural sequences children meet outside the classroom: cleaning a space methodically, scrubbing a dish from edge to center, reading an analog clock, and counting coins to make purchases. Each game uses recognizable household objects and step-by-step task structure that mirrors how an adult would teach the activity in real life.

WHAT PLAYERS BUILD HERE

Vacuuming uses a coverage-tracking grid: every square the cursor crosses changes color, so the child sees both progress and the missed areas. Wash Dishes models a circular scrubbing motion that builds wrist coordination. Read Clock cycles through hour and minute positions until both hands are read fluently. Money Math scaffolds from coin-recognition to two-step purchases (combine coins, count change).

Pairing Game With Real Activity

  1. 1Play Vacuuming in the game first, while the child watches. Discuss the grid coverage pattern — sweeping rows or columns, no gaps.
  2. 2Then have the child push a real lightweight vacuum cleaner (or a broom) over a small floor area. The pattern transfers directly. The game is a rehearsal; the real activity is the practice.
  3. 3Read Clock works the same way. Play the game for 10 minutes — then walk to a real analog clock in the kitchen and ask "what time is it?". Real-world transfer is the goal.
  4. 4Money Math: play 5–10 rounds, then bring out real coins (or play money) and run a pretend store. The bridge from screen to physical objects is what cements the skill.

Reasonable Expectations By Age

  • Age 4Recognizes that activities take time and sequence. Can recognize coins by color/size but not yet value. Reads digital clock at hour level.
  • Age 5Counts pennies and nickels. Can vacuum a small area with adult supervision. Understands "half past" on an analog clock.
  • Age 6Counts mixed coins to make small amounts ($0.25, $0.50). Reads analog clock to 5-minute precision. Can wash dishes one at a time with guidance.
  • Age 7Makes change from a dollar. Reads analog clock to 1-minute precision. Can complete a multi-step household task (sweep room, take out trash) independently.

What Trips Kids Up

  • Conflating coin size and valueNickels are physically larger than dimes but worth less. Children expect bigger = more. Verbal repetition helps: "Dimes are small but they're worth ten cents, more than a nickel." The game flags this explicitly when a wrong answer matches the size-not-value pattern.
  • Confusing the hour and minute handsThe short hand points to the hour. The long hand counts the minutes. Until this is automatic, expect mistakes. Reinforce with: "Short hand for hour, long for minute."
  • Skipping coverage areas while "cleaning"A four-year-old vacuuming a room real-world will visit only the visually prominent areas. The game enforces full coverage by tracking grid squares. This transfers: discuss the "all squares" concept and ask afterwards "did you get the corners?"
  • Money-math without contextMaking purchases is the natural application of coin counting. Bring the skill into real shopping trips early; let the child hand over the actual money and receive change. Abstract drill alone doesn't build the practical reflex.

Parent FAQ

My child can count by tens easily but struggles with mixed coins. Why?
Counting by tens is rote sequence. Coin counting is partition + addition (start at 25, add 10, add 5, add 1 = 41 cents). This combines several skills: coin recognition, decade-skip counting, single-digit addition. Master each separately before combining. Money Math's lower difficulty isolates one skill at a time.
Should kids learn digital or analog clock first?
Digital is easier (it just reads the time). Analog requires understanding fractions of a circle. Most curricula now teach digital first (age 4-5) and analog later (age 6-7). Both are necessary — analog teaches the geometry intuition that supports later math.
Is it OK to skip the housekeeping games if my child already does chores?
Yes, the household-task games are most useful for children who haven't yet been integrated into real chores. If your child already vacuums the living room weekly, the game adds little. Focus on Read Clock and Money Math instead.
