VIBRATION & ISOLATION

NATURAL FREQUENCY, TRANSMISSIBILITY, RESONANCE CHECK

OPEN VIBRATION CALCULATOR →

SCOPE OF THIS MODULE

Single-DoF vibration is governed by m̈x + cẋ + k·x = F(t). Natural frequency ωn = sqrt(k/m). For harmonic excitation at frequency ω, the transmissibility TR = (force transmitted to base) / (force applied) depends on the frequency ratio r = ω/ωn and damping ratio ζ. Vibration isolation requires r > sqrt(2); below that ratio the isolator amplifies rather than reduces force. Isolator natural frequency should be at most 1/3 of the lowest forcing frequency.

FORMULAS USED

  • ωn = sqrt(k/m)
  • f_n = (1/2π)·sqrt(g/δ_static)
  • ζ = c / (2·sqrt(k·m))
  • TR = sqrt(1 + (2ζr)²) / sqrt((1-r²)² + (2ζr)²)
  • r > sqrt(2) for isolation

References & StandardsISO 2017, Rao "Mechanical Vibrations" Ch. 3

WHERE THIS APPLIES

Mount vibrating equipment (pumps, compressors, generators) on isolators when the structure or surrounding equipment can't tolerate force or motion. Pick spring rate so f_n is well below the lowest excitation frequency. Add damping to limit amplitude during run-up through resonance. For seismic, design to a target natural frequency in the dominant earthquake band.

Worked Example: Isolating A Reciprocating Compressor

A 250 kg reciprocating compressor operates at 1200 rpm. Design rubber isolators to reduce floor force transmission to 10% of unisolated.

Excitation frequency: f_e = 1200 / 60 = 20 Hz. Target transmissibility TR = 0.10. Assume light damping (ζ ≈ 0.05 for rubber): undamped approximation TR = 1/(r² − 1) for r > sqrt(2). Solve: r² = 1 + 1/TR = 11, so r = 3.32. Isolator natural frequency: f_n = f_e / r = 20 / 3.32 = 6.0 Hz.

Required isolator stiffness: k_total = (2π·f_n)² · m = (37.7)² · 250 = 355,000 N/m. With 4 isolators in parallel: k_each = 89,000 N/m. Static deflection: δ_static = (m·g)/k_total = 2450 / 355,000 = 6.9 mm. Check against the rule-of-thumb chart: f_n = 6 Hz corresponds to ~7 mm static deflection — consistent.

With damping correction (ζ = 0.05): TR = sqrt(1 + (2·0.05·3.32)²) / sqrt((1−11)² + (2·0.05·3.32)²) = sqrt(1.110) / sqrt(100.110) = 0.105. Close to target 0.10 — design accepted. Verify isolator load capacity ≥ 1.5·(m·g/4) = 920 N each for the rubber pad rating.

Common Pitfalls

  • Confusing damped natural frequency with undampedFor typical isolator ζ < 0.1, the difference between ω_d and ω_n is <0.5%. Above ζ = 0.3, the damped frequency drops noticeably. For most isolation work, treat them as equal.
  • Designing to a resonance peak instead of through itStartup transients pass through resonance — isolators amplify load briefly. Add damping (rubber, oil snubbers) to limit the resonance peak to 5× or less. For machinery that starts and stops frequently, this is essential.
  • Ignoring the second-floor effectAn isolated machine on an upper floor sees the building's own vibration as input. If the building floor itself has f_n near the machine's isolation frequency, isolation backfires. Verify floor stiffness or use double-stage isolation.
  • Picking isolators by mass onlySame mass on stiffer springs gives higher f_n. Specify both the mass and the required static deflection (or f_n) to your isolator vendor. "350 kg machine" alone is insufficient.
  • Missing the rocking modeRigid-body vibration has 6 DoF: 3 translation, 3 rotation. Translation frequencies depend on stiffness, but rotational frequencies depend on stiffness × moment-arm² / I. For tall machines with low isolators, rocking modes can be problematic; place isolators symmetrically and verify all six natural frequencies.

Standards Detail

ISO 2017 covers mechanical vibration of resiliently mounted machinery, including isolator selection methodology and acceptance criteria.

ISO 10816 (machinery vibration assessment) defines vibration severity classes A–D for various machine sizes. Class A is "good," D is "unacceptable." Field measurement against this standard documents whether installed isolation meets specification.

For seismic design, IBC 1613 and ASCE 7 govern isolator selection in buildings. Earthquake input is broadband (1–10 Hz typical), so machine isolators tuned for 20 Hz excitation must also survive seismic without losing the machine.

Engineer FAQ

When should I use active vibration isolation?
When passive isolation can't meet the spec: large machine on a flexible floor, ultra-precise positioning (lithography, microscopy), or low-frequency excitation below 5 Hz where passive springs would need impractical static deflection.
What's the difference between a tuned mass damper and an isolator?
Isolator decouples a machine from its mount — one mass, one spring, low f_n. Tuned mass damper attaches a small auxiliary mass-spring-damper to a vibrating structure tuned to its resonance frequency, drawing energy out of the primary mode. Used in skyscrapers (Taipei 101), bridges, and tall stacks.
How do I measure damping ratio in service?
Excite the system (hammer impulse or sudden release) and record decaying oscillation. Damping ratio ζ = (1/2π)·ln(x_n / x_{n+1}) for adjacent peaks. Alternatively, sweep input frequency and identify half-power bandwidth: ζ = (f₂ − f₁) / (2f_n).
