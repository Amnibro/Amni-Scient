BATTERY PACK SIZING

WH / KWH, RUNTIME, PEUKERT, SOC, SERIES-PARALLEL

OPEN BATTERY CALCULATOR →

WHAT IT COMPUTES

Battery capacity is rated in Ah (current×time) or Wh (Ah×voltage). Pack voltage = cell voltage × series count; pack capacity = cell capacity × parallel count. Runtime under load follows Peukert's law: t = C·(C/I)^(k-1) where k is the Peukert exponent (1.0 for ideal, 1.1-1.3 for lead-acid, ~1.05 for lithium). State of charge (SOC) decreases linearly with discharged Ah for lithium, nonlinearly for lead-acid. Maximum continuous current (C-rate) limits power output and life.

KEY EQUATIONS

  • Wh = V · Ah
  • t = C / I (ideal)
  • t_actual = C · (C / I)^(k-1) (Peukert)
  • SOC = (Ah_remaining / Ah_rated) · 100
  • V_pack = V_cell · N_series; Ah_pack = Ah_cell · N_parallel

References & StandardsIEC 61960 (Li-ion), IEEE 485 (Pb-acid sizing), SAE J1715

WHEN THIS IS THE RIGHT TOOL

Size battery banks for off-grid power, electric vehicles, UPS, mobile equipment. Compute required capacity from load profile and runtime target. Pick chemistry based on energy density (Li-ion ~250 Wh/kg, Pb-acid ~40 Wh/kg), cycle life (Li-ion 1000-5000 cycles, Pb-acid 200-500), and cost. For series strings, balance cells to prevent overcharge or deep discharge of weak cells. BMS is mandatory for Li-ion above small-format.

Worked Example: Off-Grid Solar Battery Bank

An off-grid cabin uses 4 kWh/day of energy with peak load 1.5 kW. Size a lithium-iron-phosphate (LFP) battery bank for 3 days of autonomy without solar input, with 80% allowed depth of discharge (DoD).

Required energy storage: E = 4 kWh/day · 3 days / 0.80 DoD = 15 kWh usable. Pick a 48 V nominal LFP pack (16 cells in series at 3.2 V each, fully charged 3.65 V × 16 = 58.4 V). At 51.2 V nominal: Ah_required = 15,000 / 51.2 = 293 Ah.

Order four 100 Ah, 48 V LFP modules in parallel (or one 400 Ah module): 400 Ah × 51.2 V = 20.5 kWh total, 16.4 kWh usable at 80% DoD — 10% margin. Peak load check: I_peak = 1500 / 51.2 = 29 A. At 0.07 C-rate, well below LFP's 1 C continuous limit. Compatible inverter: 1500 W / 51.2 V = 30 A DC input, well within typical 48 V inverter ratings.

Cycle life: at 80% DoD, LFP cells typically deliver 3000–5000 cycles to 80% capacity. With one cycle/day, that's 8–14 years of life — battery cost amortizes over a decade. For comparison, a lead-acid AGM bank at 50% DoD would require ~30 kWh nominal (50% larger), achieve 500–1000 cycles, and need replacement in 2–4 years — usually losing on total cost of ownership despite lower upfront cost.

Common Pitfalls

  • Mixing lead-acid and lithium pack sizing approachLead-acid: derate for Peukert exponent (1.1–1.3) at high discharge currents — effective capacity drops dramatically. Lithium: nearly Peukert-flat (k ≈ 1.05) up to 1 C continuous. Mis-applying lead-acid Peukert curves to lithium under-predicts available energy by 20–40%.
  • Ignoring cell balancingCells in series with mismatched capacity cause weak cells to over-discharge or strong cells to over-charge. BMS is mandatory for lithium — passive balancing for small packs, active balancing for large stationary storage. Without BMS, the pack life is limited by the weakest cell.
  • Underestimating thermal managementDischarge at 1 C generates ~3% of stored energy as heat. For 100 kWh pack at 1 C, that's 3 kW continuous heat — needs active cooling. Without it, cell temperature rises 1°C/min and accelerates aging. EV packs use liquid cooling for this reason.
  • Forgetting end-of-life capacityBattery "end of life" is typically defined as 80% of initial capacity. Size the pack to deliver design energy at 80% capacity, not at fresh capacity — otherwise the system fails capacity test in year 5.
  • Ignoring round-trip efficiency in energy sizingLithium: 92–96% round-trip DC-DC. Lead-acid: 80–85%. Inverter adds another 5–8% loss. Daily AC load of 4 kWh requires 4/(0.94·0.93) = 4.6 kWh DC charging energy. Solar array must be sized accordingly.

Why Peukert's Law Exists

Peukert quantifies how lead-acid batteries deliver less total Ah at higher discharge currents. The underlying cause is ion-transport limitation: at high discharge rates, sulfate ions can't diffuse to the electrode surface fast enough, leaving recoverable capacity behind. The Peukert exponent k = 1 indicates ideal behavior; typical AGM batteries are k ≈ 1.15, flooded ≈ 1.20, gel ≈ 1.10.

Lithium chemistries have much higher ionic mobility and thinner electrodes, so Peukert deviation is small (k ≈ 1.03–1.07). Modern lithium polymer batteries achieve nearly ideal Peukert behavior up to 5 C continuous discharge. This is one major reason lithium dominates electric vehicles and power tools — the entire rated capacity is available at the high currents these applications demand.

Engineer FAQ

LFP vs NMC — when does LFP win?
LFP wins on safety (no thermal runaway under abuse), cycle life (3000–5000 vs 1000–2000), and cost ($/kWh). NMC wins on energy density (250 Wh/kg vs 160 Wh/kg) and cold-weather performance. Stationary storage and many commercial EVs: LFP. Long-range premium EVs and weight-critical applications: NMC or NCA.
How do I prevent lithium plating during fast charging?
Lithium plating occurs when graphite anode is overpolarized to negative potentials, typically at low temperature or high charge rate. Mitigations: (1) limit charge rate below 0.5 C at <0°C, (2) preheat pack before fast-charging in cold, (3) use anodes with lower plating risk (silicon-graphite blends, titanate). Plating is irreversible capacity loss and creates dendrite shorts.
When should I use parallel cells vs higher-capacity cells?
Parallel for redundancy, current sharing in high-current applications, and matching specific application form factors. Single high-capacity cells are simpler (one BMS channel per cell), have better thermal management, and avoid current-imbalance issues. Most modern EVs use larger cells in fewer parallel groups than early designs.
