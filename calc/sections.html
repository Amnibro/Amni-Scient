CROSS-SECTION PROPERTIES

AREA, CENTROID, MOMENT OF INERTIA, SECTION MODULUS, RADIUS OF GYRATION

OPEN SECTIONS CALCULATOR →

WHAT IT MODELS

Cross-section properties are the geometric inputs to almost every structural calculation: area A, centroid ȳ, second moment of area I, section modulus S = I/c, polar moment J, and radius of gyration r = sqrt(I/A). For composite shapes, the parallel-axis theorem (I = I_local + A·d²) lets you combine simple primitives. This module supports rectangle, circle, hollow tube, I-beam, channel, T-section, and arbitrary polygons.

CORE FORMULAS

  • A = ∫ dA
  • ȳ = (∫ y·dA) / A
  • I = ∫ y² dA
  • S = I / c
  • r = sqrt(I / A)
  • J = Ix + Iy (polar)
  • I_total = I_local + A·d² (parallel-axis)

Why The Moment Of Inertia Matters

The second moment of area I governs how efficiently a cross-section resists bending. Stress under a bending moment M is σ = M·c/I, where c is the distance from the neutral axis to the extreme fiber. Doubling I at fixed c halves the bending stress; deflection scales as 1/I as well. This is why structural sections (I-beam, channel, hollow tube) place material as far from the neutral axis as possible — mass at the centroid contributes nothing to I.

For columns, the radius of gyration r = sqrt(I/A) sets buckling resistance through the slenderness ratio KL/r. Two sections with identical area but different I behave very differently under compression. For torsion, the polar moment J = Ix + Iy of a thin-walled closed tube uses the enclosed-area shortcut: J ≈ 4·A_enc² / (∮ ds/t), which is far larger than the corresponding open section — one reason hollow shafts dominate over solid shafts of equal weight.

Worked Example: Composite T-Section

A welded T-section consists of a 100 mm × 20 mm flange sitting on top of a 80 mm × 15 mm web. Find the centroid and moment of inertia about the horizontal centroidal axis.

Areas: A_flange = 100×20 = 2000 mm², A_web = 80×15 = 1200 mm², A_total = 3200 mm². Measuring y from the bottom of the web: flange centroid at y_f = 80 + 10 = 90 mm, web centroid at y_w = 40 mm. Composite centroid: ȳ = (2000·90 + 1200·40) / 3200 = 71.25 mm.

Local moments of inertia about each part's own centroid: I_f,local = (100·20³)/12 = 66,667 mm⁴, I_w,local = (15·80³)/12 = 640,000 mm⁴. Apply the parallel-axis theorem with distance from composite centroid: d_f = 90 - 71.25 = 18.75 mm, d_w = 71.25 - 40 = 31.25 mm. Total: I = (66,667 + 2000·18.75²) + (640,000 + 1200·31.25²) = 770,000 + 1,812,500 = 2.58×10⁶ mm⁴. Section modulus at the bottom fiber: S = I / ȳ = 36,200 mm³.

Common Pitfalls

  • Forgetting the parallel-axis termI_local is always about the part's own centroid. Composite I demands A·d² for every part with a centroid offset from the composite centroid. The d² term often dominates for thin flanges far from the neutral axis.
  • Mixing units within a single calculationA cross-section measured in mm with I in mm⁴ gives stress in MPa when M is in N·mm. Slip in N·m and the answer is off by 10⁶. Track units explicitly — the calc module assumes consistent SI throughout.
  • Using rolled-section tabulated I without checking axisAISC tables list Ix and Iy separately. For an I-beam oriented with the web vertical, bending under a vertical load uses Ix (the larger value). Lay the beam on its side and Iy applies, which can be 5× smaller.
  • Treating a thin-walled open section as closed for torsionA C-channel resists torsion via warping, not the closed-tube formula. Its torsional stiffness is orders of magnitude less than a closed box of the same enclosed area. For torsion-critical applications, choose a closed section or weld a closure plate.

Engineer FAQ

When should I use S (section modulus) vs Z (plastic section modulus)?
Use S = I/c when you size for first yield (elastic design). Use Z when you allow the section to plastify before failure (plastic design, used in modern steel codes like AISC LRFD). Z is typically 12–18% larger than S for rolled shapes; the ratio Z/S is called the shape factor.
How do I compute the centroid of an arbitrary polygon?
Use the shoelace formula: ȳ = (1/6A)·Σ(y_i + y_{i+1})·(x_i·y_{i+1} − x_{i+1}·y_i). For closed polygons, sum across all edges with the last edge wrapping to the first vertex. The calc module accepts a vertex list directly for non-standard shapes.
Why does Roark list different I formulas for the same shape?
Roark tabulates I about multiple axes (geometric centroid, neutral axis, edge axis) for the same shape. Always check which axis the listed formula references before using it. The "principal" axes are aligned with the shape's symmetry; off-axis bending requires Ixx, Iyy, and the product of inertia Ixy.
