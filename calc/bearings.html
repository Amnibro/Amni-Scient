ROLLING BEARINGS (ISO 281 L10)

DYNAMIC LOAD, EQUIVALENT LOAD, L10 LIFE

OPEN BEARINGS CALCULATOR →

WHAT YOU CAN CALCULATE

Rolling-element bearing fatigue life follows the L10 model: 90% of identical bearings under identical load survive at least L10 hours. L10 = (C/P)^p · 10^6 / (60·n) where C is dynamic load rating, P is equivalent load, n is rpm, and p = 3 for ball bearings, 10/3 for roller. For combined radial and axial loading, P = X·Fr + Y·Fa with X and Y from manufacturer tables based on Fa/Fr ratio.

KEY EQUATIONS

  • L10 = (C / P)ᵖ
  • p = 3 (ball), 10/3 (roller)
  • L10h = L10 · 10⁶ / (60·n) hours
  • P = X·Fr + Y·Fa
  • L_na = a1·a23·L10 (modified)

References & StandardsISO 281, ISO 76 (static), SKF/FAG/Timken catalogs

WHEN THIS IS THE RIGHT TOOL

Pick bearings for any rotating equipment: motors, gearboxes, conveyors, machine spindles. Compute equivalent load from external forces, look up dynamic capacity from a bearing catalog, and verify L10 against the design service life (commonly 20,000-50,000 hours for industrial). For lightly loaded bearings, use the static capacity rating instead. Adjust L10 for reliability (L5, L1) using the modified rating-life equation.

Worked Example: Gearbox Output Bearing

A gearbox output shaft runs at 600 rpm carrying 8 kN radial and 2 kN axial load. Required L10 life: 30,000 hours. Select a deep-groove ball bearing.

For a deep-groove ball bearing, the equivalent load formula uses X and Y factors that depend on Fa/Fr and the bearing-specific e value. Try a candidate: SKF 6310 (bore 50 mm, C = 65 kN, e ≈ 0.24 at this Fa/Fr ratio). Compute Fa/Fr = 0.25, slightly above e, so X = 0.56, Y = 1.8.

Equivalent load: P = 0.56·8000 + 1.8·2000 = 4480 + 3600 = 8080 N. Basic dynamic capacity ratio: C/P = 65000/8080 = 8.04. Life: L10 = 8.04³ = 520 million rev. In hours: L10h = 520×10⁶ / (60·600) = 14,450 hr. Insufficient.

Upgrade to SKF 6312 (C = 81.9 kN). Same X, Y: P = 8080 N. L10 = (81.9/8.08)³ = 1040 Mrev = 28,900 hr. Still short of 30,000. Either accept slightly lower life (close enough at 96%), or jump to 6314 (C = 102 kN): L10 = 2030 Mrev = 56,500 hr — well above target. Cost-benefit: 6312 is the realistic choice; the 30,000 hr target is statistical, not absolute.

Why The Cube Law

Rolling fatigue obeys a power law because surface and subsurface stresses scale with load, but fatigue life scales with stress to the (9–10)th power (Wöhler curve). When integrated over the Hertzian contact and weighted by maximum subsurface shear depth, the result reduces to L ∝ 1/P^p where p = 3 for point contact (balls) and p = 10/3 for line contact (rollers). The cube law is empirical, but it tracks decades of bearing-fatigue test data extremely well.

The "L10" terminology comes from probability: 90% of identical bearings survive at least L10 hours under identical loads. The median life (L50) is approximately 5× L10 for ball bearings, 4× for roller. Manufacturers tabulate C ratings such that L10 = 1 million revolutions when P = C exactly.

Common Pitfalls

  • Mixing dynamic capacity C with static capacity C₀C is the rotating-shaft fatigue rating, used in the L10 formula. C₀ is the static rating, used for very slow or stationary loads where Brinell denting limits life. For oscillating bearings (rocker arms, robotic joints), use C₀ with a safety factor of 2–4.
  • Forgetting the load-zone effect on preloadPreloaded bearings (paired angular-contact) have the inner ring pushed against rollers from one side. Reduce L10 by a factor of 0.5–0.8 for typical preloads; check manufacturer's preload tables for specific values.
  • Treating belt tension as steady radial loadV-belt and flat-belt drives apply preload that doesn't change with torque, plus a tension differential that does. Use the higher (tight-side) tension for bearing sizing, and include the belt's starting and shock-overload factors.
  • Ignoring lubricant viscosity at temperatureBearings rated for grease at 40°C lose 30–50% of life when operating at 80°C because lubricant film thickness drops with viscosity. Use ISO viscosity grades matched to operating temperature; for hot environments, choose VG 100 or higher base oil.

Engineer FAQ

When should I use tapered roller instead of deep-groove ball?
Tapered roller handles combined radial + axial load directly — the cone angle resolves both into pure roller-bearing contact. Use them in pairs (face-to-face or back-to-back) for shafts under both load components. Deep-groove balls handle modest axial through curvature of the raceway and are limited to ~25% of static capacity in axial.
What's the difference between C, Cr, and C₀?
C and Cr are the same: basic dynamic load rating, in N or kN. C₀ (or C0r) is the static rating, also in N. SI catalogs use C and C₀; older Imperial catalogs sometimes used different conventions. Always check units before plugging into a life equation.
How do I size for shock loads?
Apply a service factor f_s to multiply the steady-state load. ISO 281 references factors from 1.0 (smooth operation) to 4.0 (severe shock). For belt drives use 1.2–1.5; for reciprocating machinery 2.0–3.0; for impact tools 3.0–4.0. Confirm the manufacturer's factor table for the specific application.
