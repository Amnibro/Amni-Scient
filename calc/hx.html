HEAT EXCHANGERS (LMTD & NTU)

LMTD, NTU-EFFECTIVENESS, FOULING, PARALLEL/COUNTER/CROSS

OPEN HX CALCULATOR →

WHAT THIS MODULE SOLVES

Heat exchanger duty Q = U·A·ΔT_LM where ΔT_LM is the log-mean temperature difference. Counter-flow yields the highest ΔT_LM, parallel-flow the lowest, cross-flow intermediate (apply F-factor correction). When outlet temperatures are unknown but UA and inlet conditions are, use the NTU-effectiveness method: NTU = UA/Cmin, capacity ratio Cr = Cmin/Cmax, then look up effectiveness ε from the appropriate flow-arrangement chart and compute outlets.

GOVERNING EQUATIONS

  • Q = U·A·ΔT_LM
  • ΔT_LM = (ΔT₁ - ΔT₂) / ln(ΔT₁/ΔT₂)
  • NTU = UA / Cmin
  • Cr = Cmin / Cmax
  • ε = Q / Q_max = Q / (Cmin·ΔT_max)

References & StandardsTEMA Standards, Kakac "Heat Exchangers", Incropera Ch. 11

REAL-WORLD USE CASES

Use for sizing and rating shell-and-tube, plate, double-pipe, and air-cooled exchangers. LMTD method when all four inlet/outlet temperatures are known (sizing). NTU method when only inlet temperatures are known (rating). Account for fouling resistance R_f on both sides; fouling can dominate U after a few months of service. Counter-flow over parallel-flow whenever possible.

Worked Example: Counter-Flow Sizing

Cool 2 kg/s of process water from 80°C to 45°C using 1.5 kg/s of cooling water entering at 25°C. Assume counter-flow, U = 1200 W/m²·K. Find the heat exchanger area.

Hot-side duty: Q = ṁ·cp·ΔT = 2·4180·(80−45) = 293 kW. Cold-side outlet: T_co = T_ci + Q/(ṁc·cp) = 25 + 293,000/(1.5·4180) = 25 + 46.8 = 71.8°C.

Counter-flow ΔT_LM: ΔT₁ = 80 − 71.8 = 8.2°C (hot-in vs cold-out), ΔT₂ = 45 − 25 = 20°C (hot-out vs cold-in). ΔT_LM = (20 − 8.2) / ln(20/8.2) = 11.8 / 0.891 = 13.2°C.

Area: A = Q / (U·ΔT_LM) = 293,000 / (1200·13.2) = 18.5 m². For a 25 mm OD tube exchanger, 4 m tube length: tube count = 18.5 / (π·0.025·4) = 59 tubes. With shell-and-tube layout, that's a small unit, perhaps 250 mm shell diameter.

If parallel flow were used instead: ΔT₁ = 55°C, ΔT₂ = 20.2°C, ΔT_LM = 34.7°C. Area would drop to 7.0 m² — but counter-flow is preferred because the cold outlet can exceed the hot outlet, allowing tighter temperature approach. With parallel flow, cold outlet is limited to less than hot outlet (45°C ceiling here), and this duty would be unfeasible.

Common Pitfalls

  • Forgetting the F-factor for cross-flowLMTD method assumes pure counter or parallel. Cross-flow and multi-pass shell-and-tube need F correction: ΔT_eff = F·ΔT_LM. F < 1 always; for 1-shell-2-tube-pass at R=1, F ≈ 0.85–0.95.
  • Ignoring fouling resistanceNew exchanger U-value drops 30–60% within months of service due to scaling, biological growth, and corrosion. Always include fouling factor R_f on both sides per TEMA Table RGP-T-2.4 when sizing for design life.
  • Treating phase change like sensible heatBoiling and condensing have effectively infinite heat capacity ratio; one side stays at constant temperature, ΔT_LM simplifies. But h-coefficients are very different (5,000–50,000 for condensing vs 500–5,000 for sensible) — can't use the same correlations.
  • Mismatching tube material to fluidCooling water with chlorides will pit 304SS; needs 316SS or higher. Seawater needs titanium or Cu-Ni 90/10. Always check material compatibility with the actual fluid composition, not just generic "water."
  • Skipping the pressure-drop budgetTighter passes / smaller diameters increase h but also pressure drop. For HVAC chillers, fan or pump power matters more than capital cost over the life cycle. Iterate on h and ΔP together to find the optimum.

NTU vs LMTD: When Each Wins

Use LMTD when you know all four inlet/outlet temperatures and need to size the exchanger (find A). The math is direct: compute ΔT_LM, divide Q by U·ΔT_LM.

Use NTU-effectiveness when you know only the inlet conditions and have a fixed UA — you need to predict outlet temperatures. The two unknowns make LMTD iterative; NTU gives direct answer through dimensionless variables NTU = UA/C_min and capacity ratio Cr = C_min/C_max. The effectiveness ε comes from a lookup or chart specific to the flow arrangement.

For phase-change one side, C_max → ∞ so Cr → 0. The effectiveness reduces to ε = 1 − exp(−NTU) regardless of arrangement. This is why condensers and evaporators have simpler analysis than sensible-sensible exchangers.

Engineer FAQ

Plate vs shell-and-tube — when does plate win?
Plate exchangers have 3–5× higher h than shell-and-tube due to turbulent corrugated flow. They win when: (a) tight temperature approach needed (1–5°C), (b) compactness matters, (c) one or both streams are clean. They lose for: (a) high pressure (most plates limited to 25 bar), (b) dirty service (gaskets and small clearances foul), (c) phase change with large vapor volume.
How tight a temperature approach is realistic?
5°C is comfortable, 2°C is tight, 1°C is the practical lower bound. Below 1°C, ΔT_LM → 0 and A → ∞. For very tight approach, use counter-flow and consider multiple shells in series.
When should I use a heat-pipe exchanger?
Cross-contamination must be impossible (different chemicals, sterile environments), or you need rapid response/start-stop cycles. Heat-pipes have effective conductivities 1000× copper but cost more and limit operating temperature to ~250°C with standard working fluids.
