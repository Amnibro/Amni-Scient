When To Linearize vs Solve Exactly

Engineering math is often the art of knowing when an approximation is good enough. Small-angle approximations (sin θ ≈ θ for |θ| < 0.2 rad) reduce nonlinear pendulum and beam problems to linear ODE's with closed-form solutions. Linearization breaks down at large amplitude, where you need elliptic integrals (pendulum) or numerical solution (large-deflection beam).

The standard test: if your variable stays within ±20% of the nominal operating point, linearize. Beyond that, validate the linear answer against numerical solution before using it. Most control-system design assumes linear plant; gain-scheduling or model-reference adaptive control handles the larger nonlinear envelope.

Engineer FAQ

When do I need Laplace transforms vs Fourier transforms?
Laplace for transient analysis (response to a step, impulse, or arbitrary causal input). Fourier for steady-state frequency response (Bode plots, filter design, vibration spectra). Laplace handles initial conditions naturally; Fourier assumes infinite history of the signal. Both reduce ODEs to algebraic equations.
What's the difference between dot and cross product physically?
Dot product gives a scalar — the projection of one vector onto another. Work done by a force on a displacement: W = F·d. Cross product gives a vector perpendicular to both inputs — torque, angular momentum, magnetic force on a current. Dot is "how much do these align"; cross is "how much do these twist around each other."
Why do engineers use j for imaginary unit instead of i?
Electrical engineering reserves i for current. Using i for both current and the imaginary unit creates ambiguity in equations like V = jωL·I. By using j for sqrt(−1), there's no overlap. Mathematicians and physicists outside EE still use i.

INPUTS, UNITS & ASSUMPTIONS

The Math tab is five solvers that work on plain numbers. No units are attached; keep inputs consistent. Each solver can print its steps (SHOW WORK).

  • Algebra: quadratic roots from the discriminant, including complex pairs and the vertex; cubic roots by Cardano's method, with the trigonometric form when there are three real roots; 2×2 and 3×3 linear systems by Cramer's rule.
  • Geometry: circle, sphere, cylinder, cone, rectangle, triangle (base-height area plus Heron's formula from three sides), ellipse (Ramanujan perimeter) and torus.
  • Calculus works on polynomials entered as comma-separated coefficients, highest power first. Derivatives use the power rule; definite integrals are exact from the antiderivative and cross-checked with Simpson's 1/3 rule on 1000 intervals. Limits evaluate f at x₀ ± ε for ε from 0.1 down to 0.00001.
  • Taylor series cover sin x, cos x, ex and ln(1+x). The coefficients are the Maclaurin (a = 0) values; the CENTER field does not change them in the current build.
  • Linear algebra: determinant (cofactor expansion), inverse (Gauss-Jordan above 2×2), multiply and transpose for 2×2 to 4×4. Eigenvalues are 2×2 only.
  • ODEs: first-order linear with constant P and Q (integrating factor), separable of the form dy/dx = a·xn, second-order homogeneous with constant coefficients (real, repeated or complex roots), and Euler's method for dy/dx = a·x + b·y.

Example: the default Euler problem is dy/dx = x − y with y(0) = 1, solved to x = 2 in 20 steps (Δx = 0.1). The tool returns y(2) = 1.2432. The exact solution is y = x − 1 + 2e−x, which gives 1.2707, so the error is 2.2%. With 200 steps the tool returns 1.2680 and the error drops to 0.21%. A tenfold smaller step cuts the error tenfold, as expected for a first-order method.
