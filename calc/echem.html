ELECTROCHEMISTRY (NERNST, TAFEL, BUTLER-VOLMER)

REVERSIBLE POTENTIAL, OVERPOTENTIAL, CORROSION RATE, FARADAY

OPEN ECHEM CALCULATOR →

SCOPE OF THIS MODULE

Cell potential under non-standard conditions follows the Nernst equation: E = E° - (RT/nF)·ln Q where Q is the reaction quotient. Activation overpotential at an electrode follows the Butler-Volmer equation; in the high-overpotential limit it reduces to the Tafel equation η = a + b·log|i|. Corrosion rate is computed from corrosion current density i_corr by Faraday's law: rate (mm/yr) = 3.27×10⁻³ · i_corr · EW / ρ, with i_corr typically extracted from Tafel-extrapolation or LPR measurements.

GOVERNING EQUATIONS

  • E = E° - (RT/nF) · ln Q (Nernst)
  • i = i₀[exp(αaFη/RT) - exp(-αcFη/RT)] (Butler-Volmer)
  • η = a + b·log|i| (Tafel)
  • m = (Q·M) / (n·F) (Faraday)
  • rate (mm/yr) = 3.27e-3 · i_corr · EW / ρ

References & StandardsASTM G59 (LPR), ASTM G102 (calc), Bard & Faulkner Ch. 3

REAL-WORLD USE CASES

Use for fuel cells, electrolyzers, batteries, corrosion analysis, electroplating, sensor design. The Nernst equation gives the open-circuit voltage of any electrochemical cell as a function of reactant activity. Tafel slopes characterize electrode kinetics; their crossover gives i_corr in a corroding system. Faraday's law converts current to mass deposited or consumed (essential for plating thickness control).

Worked Example: PEM Fuel Cell Open-Circuit Voltage

Compute the theoretical open-circuit voltage of an H₂/O₂ PEM fuel cell at 80°C operating on 1 atm dry hydrogen and air (P_O₂ = 0.21 atm). Standard cell potential E° = 1.229 V.

Cell reaction: H₂ + ½O₂ → H₂O. Reaction quotient: Q = P_H₂O / (P_H₂ · P_O₂^0.5). For water as liquid product, a_H₂O ≈ 1 in pure form. Take partial pressure of water vapor at 80°C ~ 0.47 atm (saturated). For 100% RH input air streams: P_H₂ = 1 atm, P_O₂ = 0.21 atm.

Nernst: E = E° − (RT/nF)·ln Q. For n = 2: RT/2F at 353 K = 8.314·353/(2·96,485) = 0.01521 V. Q = 1 / (1·0.21^0.5) = 2.18. E = 1.229 − 0.01521·ln(2.18) = 1.229 − 0.01521·0.779 = 1.217 V.

Real-world OCV is lower (~1.0–1.05 V) due to hydrogen crossover through the membrane, mixed-potential effects at the cathode, and small parasitic reactions. The Nernst calculation gives the thermodynamic ceiling; real cells achieve 80–90% of it at OCV and progressively less at increasing load due to activation, ohmic, and concentration overpotentials.

Common Pitfalls

  • Mixing standard and applied reaction directionsE° in tables is the reduction potential. For an oxidation half-reaction, flip the sign. Cell EMF = E°(cathode) − E°(anode), where both are written as reductions. Misreading this gives the wrong sign on Δ G — whole cell would be predicted to go backwards.
  • Ignoring activity vs concentrationNernst uses activity, not molar concentration. For dilute solutions activity ≈ concentration. For concentrated electrolytes (typical brines, battery acids), activity coefficients deviate significantly (γ < 1 for cations in chloride solutions). Use the Debye-Hückel limiting law or extended forms for accurate calc.
  • Treating Tafel slope as universalTafel slope b = 2.303·RT/(αnF) depends on transfer coefficient α (typically 0.5 for simple electron transfer, 0.1–0.9 for catalyzed reactions) and number of electrons in the rate-limiting step. Slopes of 30, 60, 120, 180 mV/decade indicate different rate-limiting mechanisms. Don't assume 120 mV/decade.
  • Applying corrosion rate to single-spot measurementLinear polarization measures average corrosion current over the electrode surface. Localized attack (pitting, crevice) can have much higher local rate. For pitting susceptibility, use ASTM G48 (chloride pitting test) or potentiodynamic scan rather than LPR alone.
  • Forgetting reference electrode potential conversionSaturated calomel (SCE) = +0.244 V vs SHE. Ag/AgCl 3M = +0.197 V vs SHE. Cu/CuSO₄ = +0.318 V vs SHE. Convert all potentials to a common reference (typically SHE for thermodynamics, SCE/AgAgCl for field measurements) before comparing or computing.

Industry References

Bard & Faulkner "Electrochemical Methods: Fundamentals and Applications" is the standard graduate-level text for electrochemistry. Chapter 3 covers electrode kinetics including the Butler-Volmer equation derivation and limiting-case approximations (linear-polarization, Tafel).

ASTM G59 (Standard Test Method for Linear Polarization Resistance) defines the experimental protocol for measuring corrosion rate via LPR. ASTM G102 covers conversion from current density to mass-loss rate.

For battery R&D, Newman & Thomas-Alyea "Electrochemical Systems" provides the porous-electrode theory and dilute-solution-theory mathematics that underpin physics-based battery modeling (DFN, P2D models). Indispensable for serious lithium-ion modeling.

For fuel cells, Larminie & Dicks "Fuel Cell Systems Explained" is the accessible practical reference; O'Hayre/Cha/Colella/Prinz "Fuel Cell Fundamentals" is more rigorous.

Engineer FAQ

How does Nernst potential change with temperature?
RT/F at 25°C = 0.02569 V; at 80°C = 0.03044 V; at 200°C = 0.04076 V. The temperature dependence is approximately linear. For exothermic reactions (most batteries, fuel cells), the actual cell potential decreases with temperature (dE/dT < 0); for endothermic reactions it increases. The temperature coefficient dE/dT is measured separately from Nernst — the Nernst term is only the activity correction.
Why does my corrosion-rate calc disagree with weight-loss measurement?
LPR/Tafel methods assume uniform corrosion at a known mechanism. If localized attack (pitting) is happening, the spatially averaged i_corr from electrochemical measurement under-predicts the local penetration rate. Conversely, in passive or passivating systems, electrochemical current can include capacitive/passivation contributions, over-predicting mass loss. Cross-check with periodic gravimetric coupons.
When does Butler-Volmer reduce to Tafel?
When overpotential |η| > ~120 mV. At this point, one of the two exponentials in B-V dominates by >100×, and the equation reduces to Tafel form: log|i| = log|i₀| + η/b. For high-current corrosion, electrolysis, electroplating, you're typically in this regime.
