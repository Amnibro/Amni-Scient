THERMODYNAMIC CYCLES

CARNOT, OTTO, DIESEL, BRAYTON, VAPOR-COMPRESSION REFRIGERATION

OPEN CYCLES CALCULATOR →

WHAT YOU CAN CALCULATE

Heat-engine cycles convert thermal energy to work; refrigeration cycles do the opposite using work input. Carnot is the upper-bound efficiency η = 1 - Tc/Th between hot and cold reservoirs. Otto (gasoline IC engine) efficiency η = 1 - 1/r^(γ-1) depends on compression ratio r. Diesel adds heat at constant pressure: η = 1 - (1/r^(γ-1))·(rc^γ - 1)/[γ(rc - 1)]. Brayton (gas turbine) η = 1 - 1/rp^((γ-1)/γ) depends on pressure ratio rp. Vapor-compression: COP = Qc/W_in.

THE MATH BEHIND IT

  • η_Carnot = 1 - Tc/Th
  • η_Otto = 1 - r^(1-γ)
  • η_Brayton = 1 - rp^((1-γ)/γ)
  • COP_R = Qc / W_in
  • COP_HP = Qh / W_in = COP_R + 1

References & StandardsCengel "Thermodynamics" Ch. 9-11, ASHRAE Fundamentals Ch. 2

WHEN TO USE THIS MODULE

Use for first-pass cycle analysis and component sizing in engines, turbines, refrigerators, heat pumps. Compute ideal efficiency or COP, then derate for real-component irreversibilities (compressor isentropic efficiency 0.7-0.85, turbine 0.85-0.92). For refrigeration, COP_real ≈ 0.4-0.6 × COP_Carnot at typical evaporator/condenser temperatures.

Worked Example: Otto Cycle Efficiency Estimate

A 2.5L gasoline engine has compression ratio r = 10.5. Estimate ideal thermal efficiency and compare to actual published 36% peak BSFC efficiency. γ for combustion gas ≈ 1.32 (lower than fresh air's 1.40 due to higher Cp at combustion temperatures).

Ideal Otto: η_Otto = 1 − r^(1−γ) = 1 − 10.5^(−0.32) = 1 − 0.477 = 0.523 = 52.3%

Real efficiency is much lower because: (1) γ varies through the cycle — using γ = 1.4 throughout would overpredict to 61%, (2) finite-time combustion adds entropy not modeled in the ideal cycle, (3) heat loss to cylinder walls reduces work extraction by 10–15%, (4) friction (piston rings, valvetrain, accessories) drains 8–15% of indicated power, (5) pumping losses during intake/exhaust strokes consume 3–8% at part load.

Adding these: 52% ideal × 0.85 (heat loss) × 0.88 (friction) × 0.95 (pumping) ≈ 37% — matches published BSFC peak efficiency. The Otto cycle ceiling sets the order of magnitude; cumulative losses subtract from it.

Common Pitfalls

  • Treating γ as constant across the cycleγ depends on temperature and composition. Cold intake air γ ≈ 1.40; hot combustion gas γ ≈ 1.27–1.32. Using one value throughout can over- or underpredict efficiency by 10 percentage points. For engine analysis, use a variable-property model or pick γ matched to mean cycle temperature.
  • Confusing thermal efficiency with overall efficiencyThermal efficiency = work output / heat input. Overall efficiency includes parasitic losses (oil pump, alternator, water pump) and is 5–15 percentage points lower for IC engines and 3–5 points lower for gas turbines.
  • Ignoring back-work ratio in gas turbinesBrayton cycle compressor consumes 40–65% of turbine output as "back work." Net power = turbine power − compressor power. Aero engines have lower BWR than ground-based gas turbines because of higher turbine inlet temperatures. Misreading turbine power as net power overestimates capability dramatically.
  • Using Carnot as a targetCarnot is theoretical upper bound. Practical engines reach 50–70% of Carnot efficiency at typical reservoir temperatures. Designing to "approach Carnot" is meaningless without specifying realistic component irreversibilities.
  • Ignoring the difference between LHV and HHVIC engines and gas turbines report efficiency based on LHV (excludes condensation of water vapor in exhaust). Condensing boilers and fuel cells use HHV. Same fuel, two ways to spec efficiency — always check which basis when comparing technologies.

Why Carnot Sets The Limit

The Carnot cycle achieves the maximum possible efficiency between two heat reservoirs because every heat transfer occurs at zero ΔT (isothermal), and every work step is reversible adiabatic (isentropic). Any irreversibility — finite-time heat transfer, friction, mixing, throttling — reduces efficiency below Carnot. This bound is set by the second law of thermodynamics; no clever cycle design can break it.

Real cycles introduce losses for practical reasons: finite-time combustion (Otto, Diesel), constant-pressure heat addition with combustor pressure drop (Brayton), throttling at the expansion valve (vapor compression). Each loss appears as entropy generation, which directly subtracts from work output.

Engineer FAQ

Why is COP of a heat pump always greater than 1?
COP = useful heat delivered / electrical input. The heat pump moves ambient heat plus the compressor work into the heated space. At ARI-rated conditions, residential heat pumps achieve COP ~ 3.5: 1 kW electrical input delivers 3.5 kW heat, with 2.5 kW pulled from ambient air. COP drops with outdoor temperature; at −15°C, a typical air-source HP drops to COP ~ 1.5 (still better than electric resistance at COP = 1).
When does a Stirling engine make sense?
External combustion gives fuel flexibility (biomass, solar, waste heat) and very low NOx emissions. Stirling engines work well for combined heat and power (CHP) at 1–100 kW scale, niche solar-thermal plants, and specialized military/space applications. They lose to IC engines on power density and to gas turbines on scale.
How do refrigerants affect cycle performance?
Refrigerant choice sets the operating pressure range, latent heat at evaporator/condenser temperatures, and ozone/global-warming impact. R-410A (replaced R-22), R-32, R-1234yf (auto AC), and natural refrigerants (ammonia, CO₂, propane) are current. Selection involves operating temperature range, application safety classification, and environmental regulations.
