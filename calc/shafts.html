SHAFT DESIGN (TORSION, CRITICAL SPEED, KEYS)

TORSIONAL STRESS, TWIST ANGLE, RAYLEIGH CRITICAL SPEED, KEYWAY SIZING

OPEN SHAFTS CALCULATOR →

WHAT IT COMPUTES

Power-transmission shafts carry torque T, often combined with bending from gears or pulleys. Torsional stress τ = T·r/J reaches maximum at the outer fiber. Angle of twist θ = T·L/(J·G) limits acceptable misalignment. Rotating shafts have a critical (whirling) speed where natural frequency coincides with rotation rate, producing resonance; the Rayleigh method estimates it from static deflection. Keys transfer torque to mounted hubs; standard sizing follows AGMA/ANSI B17.1.

KEY EQUATIONS

  • τ = 16T / (π·d³) (solid shaft)
  • θ = T·L / (J·G)
  • d = [(16/πSy)·sqrt((K·M)² + (K·T)²)]^(1/3) (ASME)
  • ω_cr = sqrt(g/δ_static) (Rayleigh)

References & StandardsASME B106.1M, ANSI B17.1, AGMA 2001

WHEN THIS IS THE RIGHT TOOL

Size the shaft diameter to satisfy torsional stress, bending stress, deflection, and critical-speed constraints simultaneously. Use the ASME shaft design equation for combined loading. Operate at less than 75% of first critical speed to avoid resonance, or pass through it quickly if startup transients are unavoidable. Verify key shear stress and hub bearing stress separately.

Worked Example: Pump Drive Shaft

A 60 mm 4140 steel shaft (Sy = 655 MPa, E = 200 GPa) drives a centrifugal pump impeller. Torque 850 N·m, bending moment 1100 N·m at the critical cross-section, rotating at 1750 rpm. Verify static stress and critical speed.

Section properties: J = π·(0.060)⁴/32 = 1.27×10⁻⁶ m⁴, I = J/2 = 6.36×10⁻⁷ m⁴, c = 0.030 m. Stresses: τ = T·c/J = 850·0.030 / 1.27×10⁻⁶ = 20.1 MPa, σ_b = M·c/I = 1100·0.030 / 6.36×10⁻⁷ = 51.9 MPa. Von Mises: σ_vM = sqrt(51.9² + 3·20.1²) = 62.9 MPa. FoS = 655 / 62.9 = 10.4 — comfortable.

Critical speed: with the impeller mass (15 kg) at mid-span between bearings 600 mm apart, static deflection δ_static = (m·g·L³)/(48·E·I) = (15·9.81·0.6³)/(48·200×10⁹·6.36×10⁻⁷) = 5.20×10⁻⁵ m. Rayleigh: ω_cr = sqrt(9.81 / 5.20×10⁻⁵) = 434 rad/s = 4146 rpm. Operating speed 1750 rpm is 42% of critical — safely below the 75% rule of thumb. If speed rose to 3000 rpm, redesign would be needed.

Common Pitfalls

  • Skipping the fatigue checkRotating shafts under bending see fully-reversed stress every revolution. Static FoS of 5 means almost nothing; the relevant calculation is fatigue endurance limit with all Marin modifiers. Use the Fatigue module after sizing for static.
  • Forgetting stress concentrations at steps and keywaysA shoulder fillet with r/d = 0.05 has Kt ≈ 2.5; a standard square keyway has Kt ≈ 2.0–2.5. Multiply nominal stress by Kt before yield check, and by Kf (fatigue notch factor, slightly less than Kt) for fatigue.
  • Operating at or near critical speedResonance amplifies vibration 10–100×, destroys bearings, and breaks shafts. Stay below 75% or pass quickly above 125% of every critical speed. For multi-mode systems, compute the first 2–3 modes and verify clearance for all.
  • Misaligning the shaft and motorEven 0.05 mm parallel misalignment generates large alternating bending stress. Specify alignment tolerance (laser alignment recommended for >1 kW machinery) and verify with dial indicators on commissioning.

ASME Shaft Sizing

  1. 1Identify the critical section: typically a shoulder, keyway, or maximum-moment location.
  2. 2Compute the applied torque T and bending moment M at that section. Use the worst-case combination across the operating envelope.
  3. 3Pick stress concentration factors Kt (static) and Kf (fatigue) for the geometry. Peterson's charts give Kt; Kf = 1 + q(Kt − 1) where q is notch sensitivity.
  4. 4Apply the ASME shaft equation: d = [(16/πSy)·sqrt((Kf·M)² + (Kt·T)²)]^(1/3). This gives a static-design diameter.
  5. 5Compute critical speed via the Rayleigh method using the shaft's own weight plus all rotating attached mass. Verify operating speed clearance.
  6. 6If fatigue governs, use the Soderberg or Goodman criterion on alternating bending stress — this typically increases d by 20–40%.
  7. 7Specify keyway dimensions per ANSI B17.1 and verify key shear stress: τ_key = 2T / (D·L·w).

Engineer FAQ

How tight should a shaft-to-hub fit be?
Slip-fit (clearance 0.01–0.05 mm) for components that need disassembly with a wrench. Transition fit (interference 0–0.02 mm) for components that need tap-on/tap-off. Press fit (interference 0.02–0.10 mm) for permanent assemblies relying on friction to carry torque. Always verify ISO 286 H7/h6, H7/p6, H7/r6 etc. for the chosen fit class.
Hollow shaft vs solid shaft?
Hollow shafts of the same outer diameter weigh much less but lose only modest stiffness — an ID = 0.5·OD hollow shaft has J only 6% less than the equivalent solid. Use hollow when mass matters (high-speed machines, aerospace) or when you need to route cables/fluids through the centerline.
When do I need a tachometer shaft coupling?
Whenever a flexible coupling connects motor to driven equipment with even small angular or parallel misalignment, encoder readings can lose 1 count per revolution from windup. Use a zero-backlash bellows or disc coupling for precision applications, or a rigid sleeve coupling where alignment is mechanically guaranteed.
