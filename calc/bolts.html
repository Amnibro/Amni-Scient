BOLT TORQUE & PRELOAD

NUT-FACTOR, DETAILED FRICTION, YIELD CHECKS, PRELOAD SCATTER

OPEN BOLTS CALCULATOR →

WHAT THIS MODULE SOLVES

Bolted joints rely on preload (clamp force) to hold parts together. The relationship between applied torque T and preload F is T = K·D·F where K is the nut factor (typically 0.15-0.25 depending on lubrication). A more accurate model splits friction into thread-helix and head/nut-face components. Preload scatter is significant: torque-controlled bolting typically achieves ±25% of target preload, while angle-controlled and stretch-controlled methods are tighter.

GOVERNING EQUATIONS

  • T = K·D·F (nut-factor)
  • T = F·[(d_p/2)·tan(α+ρ) + (µ_c·d_c)/2]
  • σ_t = F / A_t
  • τ = T_thread / Z_p
  • σ_eq = sqrt(σ_t² + 3τ²)
  • F_target = 0.75 · Sy · A_t (typical)

References & StandardsVDI 2230, ISO 898-1, ASME B1.1, NASA RP-1228

REAL-WORLD USE CASES

Use this module to size fasteners for any preloaded joint: pressure-vessel flanges, structural connections, engine head bolts, or bracketed equipment. Set the target preload first (typically 70-90% of bolt yield), then back-calculate the torque. Verify that bolt stress under combined preload + external load stays below yield with adequate margin. For critical joints, prefer hydraulic tensioning or angle-of-turn methods over torque alone.

Worked Example: Flange With 8 Bolts Under Pressure

A pressure-vessel flange uses eight M16 grade 8.8 bolts (proof load 91 kN per bolt) to seal a 200 mm bore against 5 MPa internal pressure. Verify the joint design and compute the required torque using K = 0.18 (lubricated).

Bore pressure load: F_press = P·A = 5×10⁶ × π·(0.100)² = 157 kN. Per bolt: F_per = 157 / 8 = 19.6 kN. With a 70% gasket reaction factor (typical for soft gaskets) and a 1.5× margin for fluctuating loads, target preload climbs to F_pre = 19.6 × (1 + 0.7) × 1.5 / 0.8 = 62.6 kN per bolt (dividing by 0.8 absorbs preload scatter from torque control).

Check against proof load: F_pre / F_proof = 62.6 / 91 = 0.69, below the 0.75–0.85 working range, so the joint is conservative. Compute torque: T = K·D·F_pre = 0.18 × 0.016 × 62,600 = 180 N·m. With torque scatter of ±25%, actual preload will sit between 47 and 78 kN — both ends inside the proof load envelope. Specify torque sequence (cross pattern, three passes at 33%/66%/100%) to limit gasket cocking.

Design Procedure

  1. 1Identify all external loads acting on the joint: pressure, axial pull, shear, prying moment. Resolve to a per-bolt equivalent.
  2. 2Pick a target preload, typically 65–85% of bolt proof load. Lower for soft gaskets that relax; higher for hard-metal joints.
  3. 3Check the joint stiffness ratio C = K_b / (K_b + K_j). For a stiff bolt in a soft gasket joint (C ≈ 0.2), most external load is absorbed by gasket unloading, not bolt stress. For a soft bolt in stiff plates (C ≈ 0.8), the bolt sees nearly all of it.
  4. 4Compute required torque T = K·D·F_pre. Document the K value and lubricant used — "dry-as-received" K can be twice "moly-lubed" K for the same fastener.
  5. 5Specify torque sequence and pass count. Pressure-vessel flanges typically need 3–4 passes in a star pattern; structural joints often need only one or two.
  6. 6For critical joints, supplement torque with angle-of-turn (e.g., snug + 90°) or stretch measurement (ultrasonic, hydraulic tensioner). These cut scatter from ±25% to ±10% or better.

Common Pitfalls

  • Treating nut factor K as a material propertyK is a system constant capturing thread friction, head/face friction, and geometry. It changes with lubricant, plating, surface roughness, and even the operator's technique. Re-measure K when any of these change; do not copy from a generic chart.
  • Sizing for working load instead of preloadA bolted joint fails when preload drops below the working load (gasket leaks) or when fluctuating load drives the bolt into fatigue. Size for sufficient preload first, then verify static and fatigue allowable.
  • Forgetting the gasket relaxation budgetSoft gaskets (rubber, PTFE, compressed sheet) relax 10–40% over the first few thousand hours of service. Initial preload must be high enough that residual preload after relaxation still exceeds the working load.
  • Ignoring embedment in newly machined jointsMicroscopic high spots on flange faces plastically deform under the first pressurization, lowering preload 5–15%. Run a "torque-untorque-retorque" or pressure cycle before final torque for critical joints.
  • Using shear bolts under preloadFriction-grip (slip-critical) joints rely on clamping force preventing slip. If a bolt is intended to carry shear in a bearing-type joint, the design checks bolt shear and bearing stress on the plate — preload becomes secondary, but anti-loosening is still required.

Standards Notes

VDI 2230 is the most rigorous joint-design standard in use today. It accounts for joint stiffness, eccentric loading, embedment, temperature, and a dozen other factors — the result is the most accurate preload prediction available in a published document. Worth the read for any joint that's safety-critical or fatigue-loaded.

ISO 898-1 defines grade markings (8.8, 10.9, 12.9) for metric bolts. The first digit times 100 gives Sut in MPa; the product of both digits times 10 gives proof load fraction × 100. So a 10.9 bolt has Sut = 1000 MPa and proof stress 90% of yield.

NASA RP-1228 (Preloaded Joint Analysis Methodology) is freely downloadable from NASA Technical Reports Server and remains the best-illustrated walkthrough of joint-stiffness analysis available. Recommended over Shigley for anyone designing a flight or aerospace joint.

Engineer FAQ

How do I prevent loosening under vibration?
Three mechanisms: (1) keep preload high enough that joint relative motion never occurs — the primary cause of self-loosening (Junker effect). (2) Use prevailing-torque locknuts (nylock, all-metal) for moderate vibration. (3) Use a thread-locking compound (Loctite 243 medium, 263 high) for severe cases. Lock washers (split, star) are largely ineffective and discouraged by VDI 2230.
What's the difference between proof load, yield, and ultimate?
Proof load is the load below which permanent set is <12.7 μm/m, typically 85–90% of yield. Yield is the 0.2% offset stress. Ultimate is the breaking stress. Bolt manufacturers spec all three; design to proof load to keep the bolt elastic with margin.
Why does torque measurement scatter so much?
Friction varies. The thread torque + face torque = 90–95% of total input torque; the remaining 5–10% is what actually stretches the bolt and generates preload. A 10% change in friction means 50–100% change in delivered preload. This is why angle-of-turn or stretch measurement is more accurate.
When should I use a hydraulic tensioner?
Anywhere preload accuracy and uniformity matter more than installation speed: wind-turbine blade bolts, large nuclear-flange joints, racing engines, anywhere the bolt diameter exceeds about M36 (turning a wrench on a giant nut is dangerous and inaccurate).
