MACHINING: SPEEDS, FEEDS & SHOP MATH

RPM AND FEED FROM CUTTING-SPEED BANDS, PERCENT-THREAD TAP DRILLS, SHEET-METAL BEND ALLOWANCE, E140 HARDNESS CONVERSION, BOLT CIRCLES

WHAT THIS MODULE SOLVES

Four calculations cover most shop-floor arithmetic. Spindle speed comes from the cutting speed the tool/material pair supports: N = 1000·V_c/(π·D), with published band values for HSS and carbide. Table feed follows from chip load per tooth. Tap drills come from the percent-of-thread formulas — metric drill = d − p·%/76.98, unified drill = d − 0.01299·%/TPI — the same math behind every chart on every shop wall. Sheet-metal flat patterns come from the bend allowance BA = θ(R + K·t) with the K-factor locating the neutral axis. Hardness conversion interpolates the ASTM E140 steel table between HRC, HV, and HB.

FORMULAS USED

  • N = 1000·V_c / (π·D) [rpm, V_c m/min, D mm]
  • v_f = f_z · z · N [mm/min]
  • MRR = a_p · a_e · v_f / 1000 [cm³/min]
  • Tap drill (metric) = d − p·% / 76.98
  • Tap drill (unified) = d − 0.01299·% / TPI
  • BA = θ·(R + K·t), BD = 2(R+t)·tan(θ/2) − BA
  • UTS ≈ 3.45 · HB [MPa, steel]

References & StandardsMachinery's Handbook (speeds & feeds, tap drill), ASTM E140 (hardness), DIN 6935 (bending)

WHERE THIS APPLIES

Use it when the chart is not on the wall: a tool diameter between rows, a material the chart skips, a 65% tap for work-hardening stainless, a flat-pattern length the press brake operator wants before cutting, a cert quoting HV when the drawing says HRC. The bolt-circle card prints X-Y coordinates for a rotary-table-less mill and the chord distance for layout verification.

Worked Example: One Part, All Four Cards

A 1018 steel plate gets a pocket, four tapped holes, and one bent flange.

Pocket: Ø10 mm 2-flute carbide, mid-band V_c = 145 m/min → N = 1000×145/(π×10) = 4,616 rpm; at f_z = 0.05 mm: v_f = 0.05×2×4616 = 462 mm/min; a 5×4 mm cut removes 9.2 cm³/min.

Holes: M6×1.0 at 75%: drill = 6 − 1.0×75/76.98 = 5.03 → 5.0 mm. The classic check: ¼-20 UNC at 75% gives 0.250 − 0.01299×75/20 = 0.2013″ — the #7 drill, exactly as the wall chart says.

Flange: t = 2 mm, inside R = 3 mm, 90°, air-bent (K ≈ 0.33): BA = (π/2)(3 + 0.66) = 5.75 mm, setback = R + t = 5 mm, bend deduction = 4.25 mm, so legs of 50 and 80 mm cut flat at 125.75 mm.

Cert says HV 413: E140 interpolation → HRC 42, HB 391, UTS ≈ 1,349 MPa — matches the 4140 HT the drawing intended.

Dialing In a New Cut

  1. 1Start at mid-band V_c for the material/tool pair — the bands assume sharp tools and coolant. Slot cuts, long stickout, and thin walls start at the LOW end.
  2. 2Set chip load by tool diameter (roughly D/200 for steel, double for aluminum) — too little rubs and work-hardens, too much chips the edge.
  3. 3Listen and look: steady hiss and C-shaped chips mean go up 10%; squeal means more feed (not less speed); blue chips in steel mean back off V_c.
  4. 4For tapping, drop to 65-70% thread in stainless and titanium — tap torque falls by a third and the joint loses almost nothing.
  5. 5Bend a test coupon on the actual brake before cutting a nest of parts: back-solve K from the measured flat, then trust YOUR K, not the table.
  6. 6Convert hardness only within the E140 family it belongs to — the steel table lies about aluminum, copper, and austenitic stainless.

Common Pitfalls

  • Running HSS at carbide numbersThe bands differ 3-5×. Carbide speeds burn an HSS end mill in seconds — the tool material column matters more than the workpiece column.
  • Chasing 100% threadGoing from 75% to 100% thread roughly doubles tapping torque for about 5% more strip strength. Broken taps in finished parts cost more than the 5%.
  • One K-factor for every bendK drifts with R/t, tooling, and grain direction: ~0.33 air-bending tight radii, up to ~0.45 above R = 2t. On precision flats, coupon-derived K beats every published table.
  • Chip-load panic below Ø4 mmSmall tools deflect and snap at book chip loads. Halve f_z below Ø4 and use full theoretical speed — micro tools die from force, not heat.
  • Hardness-testing thin or case-hardened parts with the wrong scaleA Brinell ball punches through thin case depth and reads the soft core; HRC needs ~10× indent depth of material beneath. Use HV microhardness on cases and sheet.

Engineer FAQ

Climb or conventional milling?
Climb by default on any machine with backlash control: better finish, lower cutting force, chips thrown clear of the cut. Conventional survives on worn knee mills (backlash makes climb grab) and through scale or flame-cut skin where the down-cutting edge would land on the crust.
Why does my calculated RPM exceed the spindle?
Small tools want more speed than most spindles have — a Ø3 carbide tool in aluminum computes to 40k+ rpm. Run at max spindle and keep the CHIP LOAD correct at that reduced speed; feed per tooth, not surface speed, protects the tool.
Where does 76.98 come from in the tap-drill formula?
It is the geometry constant of a 60° thread: one full thread height H = 0.86603·p engages 76.98% when the drill leaves exactly d − p. That is why the ancient d − p rule of thumb IS the 77% drill.
Does the 3.45·HB tensile estimate replace a pull test?
No — it is a ±10% correlation for carbon and low-alloy steels used for sanity checks and incoming-material screening. Certs, code work, and anything safety-rated need the real tensile test.
