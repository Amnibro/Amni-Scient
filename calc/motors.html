INDUCTION MOTORS

SYNCHRONOUS SPEED, SLIP, FULL-LOAD AMPS, TORQUE

OPEN MOTORS CALCULATOR →

WHAT IT COMPUTES

Three-phase induction motors run near synchronous speed N_s = 120·f / P where f is line frequency and P is the pole count. Actual rotor speed N is below N_s by slip s = (N_s - N) / N_s, typically 1-5% at full load. Full-load amps (FLA) follow I_FL = P_out / (sqrt(3)·V_LL·η·PF) for three-phase. Starting current (locked-rotor) is 5-7× FLA; voltage dip during start is a common system constraint. Service factor (SF) allows brief overload (1.0 standard, 1.15 typical for premium-efficiency).

KEY EQUATIONS

  • N_s = 120 · f / P
  • s = (N_s - N) / N_s
  • I_FL = P_out / (sqrt(3) · V_LL · η · PF) (3φ)
  • T = (P_out · 9.55) / N_rpm (N·m)
  • I_LR ≈ 6 · I_FL (typical)

References & StandardsNEMA MG 1, NFPA 70 Article 430, IEEE 841

WHEN TO USE THIS MODULE

Size feeders, starters, and overload protection per NEC Article 430. Verify branch conductor ampacity at 125% of FLA, short-circuit and ground-fault protection at the appropriate multiple. For VFD-driven motors, derate for harmonic heating. Reduced-voltage starters or soft-starts limit inrush when supply impedance is constrained. Always check name-plate FLA, not table values, for the actual motor.

Worked Example: 4-Pole 50 HP Motor Sizing

A pump drive needs 37 kW at 1750 rpm. Pick a NEMA-frame three-phase induction motor and verify FLA, branch protection, and starter ratings. Supply: 480 V three-phase, η = 0.94, PF = 0.87.

4-pole synchronous speed: N_s = 120·60/4 = 1800 rpm. Slip at full load: s = (1800 − 1750)/1800 = 2.8% — typical for NEMA Design B. Pick a 50 HP (37.3 kW) NEMA 326T frame motor.

Full-load amps: I_FL = P / (sqrt(3)·V·η·PF) = 37,300 / (1.732·480·0.94·0.87) = 55.4 A. Compare to NEMA MG 1 Table 12-30: 50 HP 460V 4-pole = 65 A nameplate. Use nameplate value for code compliance.

NEC 430.22 branch conductor: I_design = 1.25·I_FL = 1.25·65 = 81.3 A. From NEC Table 310.16, copper THWN at 75°C: #4 AWG (85 A). Branch breaker per 430.52: 250% for inverse-time breaker = 162.5 A — round up to standard 175 A. Overload relay setting per 430.32: 1.15×FLA = 75 A (Class 10 or 20 depending on load type).

Locked-rotor current ≈ 6×FLA ≈ 390 A. Verify supply transformer impedance allows this without unacceptable voltage dip. For 200 kVA transformer at 5.5% Z: voltage dip during start ≈ I_LR·Z / I_rated = 390·0.055/241 = 8.9% — acceptable for occasional starts. Frequent starts may need a soft starter or VFD.

Common Pitfalls

  • Sizing only for steady-state powerMotors must start their connected load — inrush at locked rotor is 5–7× FLA. Use a soft starter or VFD for high-inertia loads, or oversize the supply transformer. Voltage dip during start must stay above 80% of nominal or contactors drop out.
  • Ignoring service factor in continuous dutyService factor (SF) indicates short-time overload capability, not continuous. A 1.15 SF motor running at 1.15·FLA continuously runs hot and loses life. For continuous overload, oversize to the next standard frame.
  • Mismatching motor type to loadNEMA Design B: general purpose (centrifugal pumps, fans). Design C: high starting torque (conveyors, positive-displacement pumps). Design D: very high starting torque + slip (shears, presses). Mismatch causes early failure or inadequate starting torque.
  • Forgetting harmonics with VFD operationVFD-driven motors see 5–7% extra heating from PWM harmonics. Use "inverter-duty" insulation rated for 1600 V peak (NEMA MG 1 Part 31) on the motor terminals, especially for long-cable runs to the drive. Derate the motor by 5–10% for VFD service unless explicitly rated.
  • Skipping power-factor correction at the motorLightly loaded induction motors have PF as low as 0.5. Adding individual PF correction capacitors at the motor terminals is more effective than a central bank because reactive current never flows in the feeder. Size capacitor to bring motor PF to 0.95–1.0 at typical load.

Standards Notes

NEMA MG 1 (Motors and Generators) is the comprehensive standard for North American electric motors. Frame sizes, performance designations (A/B/C/D), enclosure types (ODP, TEFC, TEAO, etc.), and efficiency ratings (Premium per NEMA MG 1 Table 12-12) are all defined here.

NFPA 70 (NEC) Article 430 governs motor circuit installation: conductor sizing, branch protection, motor disconnects, overload protection, and grounding. Compliance is required for any motor installation in the US.

IEEE 841 specifies "severe-duty" totally-enclosed motors for petrochemical service: bearing life, vibration, lubrication, and quality control beyond NEMA MG 1 minimums. Common spec for critical motors at chemical plants and refineries.

Engineer FAQ

When should I use a VFD instead of soft starter?
VFD when load varies and you want speed control + energy saving (pumps, fans — cubic affinity law makes VFD energy savings dramatic at partial load). Soft starter when load is constant-speed but starting torque or inrush is a problem (rock crushers, conveyors). VFDs cost 3–5× more but offer much more flexibility.
Premium-efficiency vs standard motor — payback?
Premium-efficiency motors run 1–3 percentage points more efficient than standard. At $0.10/kWh and 6000 hr/yr operation, a 50 HP motor saves ~$200/yr. Cost premium ~$400–$800 over standard. Simple payback: 2–4 years. Required by US law (EISA) for most new installations since 2010.
How do I prevent bearing fluting on VFD-driven motors?
PWM voltage induces shaft voltages that arc through bearings, electroerosion-pitting the raceways. Solutions: shaft-grounding brushes (AEGIS rings), insulated bearings on the non-drive end, or common-mode filters on the drive output. Specify when ordering, retrofit if you see fluting in service.
