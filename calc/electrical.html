OHM'S LAW, AC POWER TRIANGLE, THREE-PHASE, RC/RL TRANSIENTS

WHAT IT MODELS

DC follows V = IR, P = VI. AC adds reactance: X_L = 2πfL (inductor), X_C = 1/(2πfC) (capacitor), and impedance Z = sqrt(R² + (XL - XC)²). Power splits into real P (W), reactive Q (VAR), apparent S (VA): S = V·I, P = V·I·cosφ, Q = V·I·sinφ, where cosφ is power factor. Three-phase systems add a sqrt(3) factor: S₃φ = sqrt(3)·V_LL·I_L. RC charging follows V(t) = V_f·(1 - e^(-t/RC)).

CORE FORMULAS

  • V = I·R; P = V·I (DC)
  • Z = sqrt(R² + (XL - XC)²)
  • PF = cosφ = P/S
  • S₃φ = sqrt(3)·V_LL·I_L
  • V(t) = V_f·(1 - e^(-t/τ)), τ = RC

References & StandardsIEEE Std 141 (Red Book), IEEE 519, NFPA 70

WHEN TO REACH FOR IT

Use for any AC or DC circuit analysis: motor branch sizing, capacitor-bank power-factor correction, transformer rating, RC filter design, transient analysis of switch-on inrush. For three-phase loads, line-to-line voltage and line current relate by the configuration (wye or delta). Improve power factor with capacitors to reduce kVA demand.

Worked Example: Three-Phase Power & PF Correction

A 480 V three-phase induction motor draws 65 A line current at 0.78 power factor lagging. Compute apparent, real, and reactive power; size a capacitor bank to correct PF to 0.95.

Apparent power: S = sqrt(3)·V_LL·I_L = sqrt(3)·480·65 = 54.0 kVA. Real power: P = S·cosφ = 54.0·0.78 = 42.1 kW. Reactive power: Q_existing = S·sinφ = 54.0·sin(arccos 0.78) = 54.0·0.626 = 33.8 kVAR.

Target PF 0.95: tan(arccos 0.95) = 0.329. Target reactive: Q_target = P·tan(arccos 0.95) = 42.1·0.329 = 13.8 kVAR. Capacitor bank needed: Q_cap = Q_existing − Q_target = 33.8 − 13.8 = 20.0 kVAR.

For a delta-connected capacitor bank at 480 V: C_per_phase = Q_cap / (3·ω·V²) = 20,000 / (3·377·480²) = 76.6 μF per phase. Order three 75 μF, 480 V capacitors and verify the actual installed PF after commissioning. New line current: I_new = (P / (sqrt(3)·V·PF)) = 42,100 / (1.732·480·0.95) = 53.3 A — an 18% reduction, freeing transformer capacity and reducing I²R losses.

Common Pitfalls

  • Confusing line and phase voltagesIn a wye-connected system, V_line = sqrt(3)·V_phase. In delta, V_line = V_phase. Mixing these up gives 73% error or 73% overestimate. Always specify which voltage is used and verify the transformer secondary connection.
  • Treating peak as RMSAC values are RMS unless otherwise noted. For sine waves: V_peak = sqrt(2)·V_rms ≈ 1.414·V_rms. Insulation ratings often quote peak; transformer ratings quote RMS. Mismatch causes either premature breakdown or over-design.
  • Ignoring harmonic distortion in PF calculationNon-sinusoidal currents (from VFDs, switching power supplies) need true power factor = P/S, which is lower than displacement power factor (cosφ on fundamental). Harmonic-rich loads can show PF = 0.65 even after displacement correction; need active harmonic filters.
  • Missing transformer inrush currentWhen energized, transformers can draw 8–12× rated current for 100–500 ms. Sizing only for steady-state means breakers trip during energization. Use breakers with appropriate trip curve (typically Class C or D for transformer feeders).
  • Skipping voltage drop check for long runsNEC suggests <3% on a branch and <5% combined branch+feeder. A 100 m run at 80% rated current can drop 8% even for properly ampacity-sized cable. Use the NEC module to verify drop after sizing for ampacity.

Why AC Beat DC In The 1890s

Edison's DC distribution system was limited by I²R losses: at 110 V, supplying 100 kW takes 909 A, generating enormous resistive losses in copper wiring beyond a few kilometers. Tesla and Westinghouse's AC system used transformers to step up voltage for transmission (~10 kV early, hundreds of kV today) and back down for delivery — at 10 kV, the same 100 kW takes only 10 A, reducing wire losses by 8000×.

Transformers work only with AC. AC is also easier to interrupt (current naturally crosses zero, allowing arc extinction) and generate (rotating machinery produces sinusoidal EMF naturally). DC won the laboratory war (Edison's lab) but lost the distribution war. Modern HVDC transmission has reclaimed long-distance bulk transmission for DC because of reduced reactive-power losses, but distribution remains AC.

Engineer FAQ

When does PF correction not pay off?
When utility billing doesn't penalize PF, when load varies widely (capacitors then over-correct at light load and cause voltage rise), or when the facility has substantial harmonic content (capacitors resonate with system inductance and amplify harmonics). Active harmonic filters or static-VAR compensators are alternatives.
Is single-phase enough for residential loads?
For total connected load <30 kW (typical US single-family home with central AC), yes. Above this or for symmetric three-phase loads (motors), three-phase is more efficient. Most large commercial and all industrial installations are three-phase.
When should I use 3-phase 4-wire vs 3-wire?
4-wire (wye with neutral) when you have single-phase loads to distribute (lighting, receptacles), or when imbalance is unavoidable. 3-wire (delta or wye without neutral) for purely three-phase loads (large motors, balanced industrial), saving conductor cost. Always provide a separate equipment ground conductor regardless.
