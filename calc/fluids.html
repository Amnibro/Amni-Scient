PIPE FLOW (MOODY / BERNOULLI / REYNOLDS)

FRICTION FACTOR, ENERGY BALANCE, MINOR LOSSES

OPEN FLUIDS CALCULATOR →

WHAT IT COMPUTES

Internal pipe flow is laminar below Re=2300, turbulent above 4000. Friction factor f comes from f = 64/Re (laminar) or the Colebrook equation (turbulent), which Amni-Calc solves explicitly via Swamee-Jain or iteratively. The Moody diagram visualizes f as a function of Re and relative roughness ε/D. Bernoulli's equation accounts for pressure, velocity, and elevation head between two points, plus friction (Darcy-Weisbach) and minor losses (fittings, valves) summed via loss coefficients K.

KEY EQUATIONS

  • Re = ρ·v·D / μ
  • f = 64/Re (laminar)
  • 1/sqrt(f) = -2·log10(ε/(3.7D) + 2.51/(Re·sqrt(f))) (Colebrook)
  • h_L = f·(L/D)·v²/(2g) + ΣK·v²/(2g)
  • P₁ + ½ρv₁² + ρgz₁ = P₂ + ½ρv₂² + ρgz₂ + ΔP_loss

References & StandardsCrane TP-410, Moody (1944), White Fluid Mechanics

WHEN THIS IS THE RIGHT TOOL

Size pipes for any liquid or gas system: water distribution, hydraulic lines, compressed air, process plumbing. Compute Re first to pick laminar or turbulent regime, then friction factor, then total head loss including minor losses. Pair with the pump module to find the operating point on a system curve. For long lines, iterate on diameter to balance capital cost vs pumping cost.

Worked Example: Cooling Water Loop Pressure Drop

A cooling water loop circulates 200 L/min through 80 m of schedule 40 carbon-steel pipe (3" nominal, ID = 77.9 mm, ε = 0.046 mm), including 12 standard elbows (K = 0.75 each), 2 gate valves (K = 0.17), and a 3 m elevation rise. Compute total pressure drop. Water at 25°C: ρ = 997 kg/m³, μ = 8.9×10⁻⁴ Pa·s.

Velocity: Q = 200/60 L/s = 3.33×10⁻³ m³/s. Area = π·(0.0779)²/4 = 4.77×10⁻³ m². v = 0.699 m/s. Reynolds: Re = 997·0.699·0.0779 / 8.9×10⁻⁴ = 61,000 — turbulent. Relative roughness: ε/D = 0.046/77.9 = 5.9×10⁻⁴. Colebrook gives f ≈ 0.0234 (or Swamee-Jain: 0.0238, close).

Major losses: h_L,major = f·(L/D)·(v²/2g) = 0.0234·(80/0.0779)·(0.699²/19.62) = 0.598 m. Minor losses: ΣK = 12·0.75 + 2·0.17 = 9.34; h_L,minor = 9.34·(v²/2g) = 0.233 m. Elevation: 3 m. Total head: 0.598 + 0.233 + 3 = 3.83 m. Pressure drop: ΔP = ρ·g·h = 997·9.81·3.83 = 37.5 kPa ≈ 5.4 psi. Pick a pump rated for at least 4 m of head at 200 L/min, plus 20% margin.

Common Pitfalls

  • Using Reynolds based on hydraulic diameter for laminar flow without correctionFor non-circular ducts in laminar flow, the friction factor depends on aspect ratio — not just on hydraulic diameter. Rectangular ducts have laminar f·Re between 56 (square) and 96 (slot). Use the actual shape factor from Shah-London tables.
  • Treating laminar regime with ColebrookColebrook applies only to turbulent flow (Re > ~4000). For laminar, use f = 64/Re. The transition region (2300 < Re < 4000) is genuinely unstable and unpredictable — design either side of it, not within it.
  • Ignoring two-phase pressure dropSteam, mixed-phase refrigerant, or aerated liquids do not follow single-phase Darcy. Use Lockhart-Martinelli or homogeneous-flow correlations; pressure drop can be 5–50× the equivalent single-phase prediction.
  • Using nominal pipe ID instead of actualSchedule 40 3" pipe has nominal 3" but actual ID = 77.9 mm (3.068"). Schedule 80 same nominal size = 73.7 mm. Look up the actual ID from ASME B36.10 before computing area and velocity.
  • Forgetting K factor scaling with pipe sizeK factors in Crane TP-410 are valid for fully turbulent flow at a specific size. For very small pipes or laminar regime, K can be 2–3× higher. Use the "L_eq" equivalent-length method for size-dependent components.

Why Friction Factor Has Two Regimes

In laminar flow (Re < 2300), molecular viscosity dominates — momentum transfers between layers by molecular collision. Pressure drop scales linearly with velocity, and f = 64/Re is derived from the parabolic velocity profile (Hagen-Poiseuille). The flow is steady, predictable, and the wall roughness is irrelevant because the viscous sublayer engulfs all surface features.

In turbulent flow (Re > 4000), eddies dominate momentum transfer. The viscous sublayer is thin (a few hundred microns), and surface roughness pokes through it, dramatically increasing resistance. Pressure drop scales nearly with v². Colebrook captures the combined viscous + roughness behavior empirically; explicit approximations (Swamee-Jain, Haaland) are within 2% of Colebrook for engineering use.

The transition region 2300–4000 is bistable: small disturbances flip the flow between laminar and turbulent unpredictably. Real engineering practice avoids designing into this band — either oversize for laminar (small low-flow systems) or undersize for turbulent (mainstream industrial).

Engineer FAQ

When does compressibility matter for gas flow?
When Mach number M = v/c exceeds 0.3. Below this, treat gas as incompressible — Bernoulli + Darcy still works. Above 0.3, density changes appreciably along the pipe and you need Fanno flow (adiabatic with friction) or Rayleigh flow (frictionless with heat addition) analysis.
How do I size a pipe for water-hammer survival?
Joukowsky pressure rise from sudden valve closure: ΔP = ρ·c·Δv, where c is the wave speed (~1200 m/s in water in steel pipe). A 1 m/s velocity stop generates 1.2 MPa pressure spike — often larger than the operating pressure. Either spec the pipe wall for the surge, or limit closure rate, or install a relief valve / surge tank.
Why do I keep losing pressure at branch tees?
Branch tees have K factors of 0.5–2.0 depending on flow direction and branch-to-main flow split. Run vs. branch flow is asymmetric (K_run ≈ 0.1, K_branch ≈ 1.0–1.5). Look up the specific value in Crane TP-410 Appendix A for the actual tee geometry.
