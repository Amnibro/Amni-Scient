FATIGUE (GOODMAN, SODERBERG, GERBER)

MEAN-STRESS DIAGRAMS, ENDURANCE LIMIT MODIFIERS, INFINITE LIFE

OPEN FATIGUE CALCULATOR →

WHAT YOU CAN CALCULATE

Cyclic loading at stresses below static yield can still cause failure by fatigue. The endurance limit Se is the stress amplitude below which steel survives infinite cycles; for non-ferrous metals there's no true endurance limit. Marin modifiers reduce Se for surface finish, size, loading type, temperature, and reliability. Mean stress σ_m raises the effective amplitude; Goodman, Soderberg, Gerber, and ASME-Elliptic lines plot allowable amplitude vs mean on a Haigh diagram.

THE MATH BEHIND IT

  • Se = ka·kb·kc·kd·ke·kf · 0.5·Sut
  • Goodman: σ_a/Se + σ_m/Sut = 1/n
  • Soderberg: σ_a/Se + σ_m/Sy = 1/n
  • Gerber: n·σ_a/Se + (n·σ_m/Sut)² = 1
  • ASME-Ell: (n·σ_a/Se)² + (n·σ_m/Sy)² = 1

References & StandardsShigley Ch. 6, Norton Ch. 6, MIL-HDBK-5

WHEN TO USE THIS MODULE

Apply fatigue analysis to any cyclically loaded component: rotating shafts under bending, springs in compression cycles, pressurized vessels with cyclic pressure, structural members in vibration. Identify mean and amplitude stress separately. Use Goodman for ductile materials with a conservative bias; Soderberg if you can't accept any yielding; Gerber for less conservative match with test data; ASME-Elliptic for combined yield + fatigue safety.

Worked Example: Marin Modifier Cascade

Find the endurance limit of a machined 1045 steel shaft, 50 mm diameter, rotating in bending, machined surface (Ra ≈ 1.6 μm), at 99% reliability.

Ideal endurance limit (rotating beam, polished, 50% reliability): Se' = 0.5·Sut = 0.5·630 = 315 MPa.

Marin factors (Shigley): Surface (k_a = a·Sut^b, machined: a = 4.51, b = −0.265): k_a = 4.51·630^(−0.265) = 0.81. Size (k_b for d > 8 mm in bending: 1.51·d^(−0.107)): k_b = 1.51·50^(−0.107) = 0.83. Load (k_c = 1.0 for bending, 0.85 axial, 0.59 torsion): k_c = 1.0. Temp (k_d = 1.0 at room temp): k_d = 1.0. Reliability (k_e for 99%): k_e = 0.814. Miscellaneous (k_f for residual stress, plating, corrosion): k_f = 1.0.

Final endurance limit: Se = 0.81·0.83·1.0·1.0·0.814·1.0·315 = 173 MPa. Down from 315 MPa — almost half. This is why Marin modifiers are essential; un-corrected handbook Se overpredicts real-world life by 80–100%.

Now apply Goodman: with σ_m = 60 MPa and σ_a = 90 MPa (the actual mean and alternating bending stress at the keyway, with Kf = 1.8 already applied): 1/n = 90/173 + 60/630 = 0.520 + 0.095 = 0.615, so n = 1.63. Marginal — the keyway location needs polishing or a larger fillet to recover safety.

Common Pitfalls

  • Using rotating-beam Se as final enduranceHandbook Se = 0.5·Sut is the polished, rotating-beam, 50%-survival, room-temperature, perfect-specimen value. Real parts lose 30–60% of this through Marin modifiers. Always compute the cascade.
  • Ignoring mean stress with the simple S-N curveA pure S-N curve assumes fully-reversed loading (σ_m = 0). Real shafts under both bending and axial load have nonzero mean stress, which raises the alternating threshold for failure. Use Goodman, Soderberg, or Gerber to combine.
  • Applying Kt instead of KfKt is the elastic stress concentration factor. Kf is the fatigue notch factor, lower than Kt because real materials show notch sensitivity less than 1. Kf = 1 + q(Kt − 1) where q is notch sensitivity (Neuber or Peterson). Brittle materials approach q = 1; soft steels q ≈ 0.6.
  • Forgetting non-ferrous materials have no endurance limitAluminum, copper, and most polymers show no clean horizontal asymptote on the S-N curve. Use a fatigue strength at a specific cycle count (typically 5×10⁸ for aluminum) instead of an endurance limit.
  • Treating cumulative damage with simple stress thresholdsVariable-amplitude loading requires Miner's rule: Σ(n_i / N_i) = 1 at failure. Each stress level contributes a fraction of life consumed; tracking spectrum is essential for fatigue-critical equipment.

Why Endurance Limit Exists For Steel

Ferrous BCC iron has a dislocation pinning mechanism (interstitial carbon and nitrogen) that arrests fatigue crack growth below a threshold stress amplitude. This creates the characteristic horizontal asymptote on the S-N curve at about Sut/2. The phenomenon is unique to body-centered cubic steels with sufficient interstitial content; austenitic stainless steels (FCC, no interstitial pinning) do not show a true endurance limit and require fatigue strength quoted at a specific cycle count.

Aluminum, copper, magnesium, and most polymers all lack this pinning mechanism. Their S-N curves continue to decrease with cycle count, never plateauing. Aircraft aluminum structures are deliberately designed to a finite "safe life" or "damage tolerance" basis, not infinite life.

Engineer FAQ

Goodman vs Soderberg vs Gerber — which to use?
Goodman: balanced, slight bias to conservative; default for most ductile metals. Soderberg: most conservative (uses Sy not Sut); choose when you cannot accept any yielding. Gerber: least conservative; matches experimental data best but small errors push toward unconservative. ASME-Elliptic: elegant compromise; defaults in many design codes.
How does shot-peening improve fatigue life?
Shot-peening induces compressive residual stress at the surface, 200–400 μm deep. Mean stress at the fatigue crack initiation site becomes net compressive, which prevents crack opening. Life improvements of 2–10× are typical for steel and titanium.
When do I need to design for damage tolerance instead of safe life?
When the consequence of failure is catastrophic and inspection intervals exist: aircraft, nuclear pressure boundary, hoisting equipment. Damage tolerance assumes a crack exists at the start of life and verifies it cannot grow to critical size between inspections.
