SPUR GEARS (GEOMETRY, LEWIS BENDING)

MODULE, PITCH, RATIO, CONTACT RATIO, LEWIS BENDING STRESS

OPEN GEARS CALCULATOR →

SCOPE OF THIS MODULE

Spur gear geometry is set by the module m (or diametral pitch P_d in inch units), number of teeth N, and pressure angle (commonly 20°). Pitch diameter d = m·N. Contact ratio measures how many tooth pairs share the load at any instant; 1.4-1.8 is typical. The Lewis bending equation estimates root stress: σ = W_t / (F·m·Y) where Y is the Lewis form factor based on tooth count. Modern AGMA design adds geometry, dynamic, and surface-condition factors.

GOVERNING EQUATIONS

  • d = m · N
  • i = N₂ / N₁
  • C = (d₁ + d₂) / 2
  • σ = W_t / (F · m · Y) (Lewis)
  • m_c = (Z_a + Z_r) / p_b (contact ratio)

References & StandardsAGMA 2001, ISO 6336, ANSI B6.1

REAL-WORLD USE CASES

Size gears for any rotational power transmission: gearbox, automotive transaxle, robot joint, conveyor drive. Start with required ratio and center distance, then iterate on module to satisfy bending and contact-stress allowables. Pinion is typically critical (smaller, higher tooth-load cycles). Verify backlash, undercut for pinions with low tooth count, and lubrication for pitting resistance.

Spur Gear Design Procedure

  1. 1Define inputs: required gear ratio i = N₂/N₁, input/output speed and torque, required service life, environment (dry, splash, pressure lube).
  2. 2Choose pressure angle. 20° is standard; 25° increases bending strength at the cost of higher separation force; 14.5° survives in legacy systems but is being phased out.
  3. 3Pick module m (metric) or diametral pitch P_d (inch). Larger module = stronger but bulkier teeth. Start from a trial value of m = sqrt(W_t / (Y·σ_allow·F·Y_p)) and iterate.
  4. 4Compute pitch diameters: d₁ = m·N₁, d₂ = m·N₂. Center distance C = (d₁ + d₂)/2. Verify the centers fit in your envelope.
  5. 5Check contact ratio m_c > 1.4. Below this, the gears engage and disengage with shock; below 1.0 they momentarily lose contact and ratchet.
  6. 6Compute bending stress using AGMA equation: σ = W_t·K_o·K_v·K_s·(P_d/F)·(K_m·K_B/J). Compare to allowable.
  7. 7Compute contact (pitting) stress using AGMA equation. For typical industrial gears, pitting often governs over bending. Pinion is usually critical because it sees more cycles per unit time.
  8. 8Verify undercut for pinions with low tooth count; below 18 teeth at 20° pressure angle, profile shift is needed.

Worked Example: Reducer Pinion

Design a steel pinion for a 1000 N·m torque application. Pinion has 22 teeth, mates with a 66-tooth gear (ratio 3:1), module m = 5 mm, face width F = 50 mm. Material: AISI 4140 through-hardened (St = 380 MPa bending allowable, Sc = 1170 MPa contact allowable).

Pitch diameter pinion: d₁ = 22·5 = 110 mm. Tangential force: W_t = 2T/d₁ = 2·1000 / 0.110 = 18,180 N. Lewis form factor for 22 teeth, 20° pressure angle: Y ≈ 0.330 (from AGMA table).

Bending stress (Lewis, simplified): σ_b = W_t / (F·m·Y) = 18,180 / (0.050·0.005·0.330) = 220 MPa. Compare to St = 380 MPa with service factor K_o = 1.25 for moderate shock: σ_allow = 380/1.25 = 304 MPa. Bending OK.

Contact stress (AGMA, approximate): σ_c = Cp·sqrt((W_t·K_o·K_v / (F·d₁·I))) where Cp ≈ 191 sqrt(MPa) for steel-on-steel, I ≈ 0.10 for this geometry. With K_v = 1.20 (typical for 8 m/s pitch line velocity): σ_c ≈ 191·sqrt(18180·1.25·1.20 / (0.050·0.110·0.10)) = 191·sqrt(49,500) = 1300 MPa. Pitting allowable for through-hardened 4140 ≈ 1170 MPa — contact stress exceeds allowable. Either increase face width to 65 mm, or switch to a surface-hardened material (carburized 8620, Sc ≈ 1900 MPa).

Common Pitfalls

  • Designing only for bending strengthPitting (surface fatigue) is the most common failure mode in well-lubricated industrial gears. Always run both bending and pitting checks; pitting usually governs.
  • Forgetting backlash in precision applicationsStandard tooth-cutting tolerances leave 0.05–0.15 mm backlash per pair. For servo positioning, this is unacceptable; use anti-backlash split-gear pairs, preloaded duplex pairs, or harmonic drives.
  • Ignoring lubrication regimeOpen gears, splash-lube, and pressure-lube have very different K factors and allowable stresses. AGMA 925 covers lubrication regime selection. Dry gears (plastic-on-plastic) follow entirely different allowables.
  • Mismatched material hardnessPinion should be 1–3 Rockwell points harder than the mating gear to even out wear — the pinion sees 3× the contact cycles (in a 3:1 ratio). Match-hardened pairs wear and pit at the same rate.

Engineer FAQ

Spur vs helical — when does helical win?
Helical gears engage gradually along the tooth, lowering noise and load shock. Use them above ~10 m/s pitch line velocity, in quiet applications (HVAC, automotive), or for high power density. Penalty: axial thrust load on the bearings, which raises bearing cost.
How do I prevent gear noise?
Three levers: (1) tighter manufacturing tolerance (ground vs hobbed teeth), (2) higher contact ratio (m_c > 1.8 stays in continuous mesh), (3) helical or herringbone profile. Lubrication viscosity also matters — thicker oil dampens transmission error.
When should I use a herringbone gear?
High-power, high-precision applications where helical thrust would oversize the bearings: marine reduction, large industrial reducers, turbine generator drives. Herringbones cancel axial thrust by combining left- and right-hand helices, but they cost 3–5× more than equivalent helical gears.
