LIFTING & RIGGING: SLING LOADS

SLING TENSION BY ANGLE AND HITCH, COG-OFFSET LEG LOADS, THE 30° REFUSAL, EYEBOLT DERATES

OPEN RIGGING CALCULATOR →

WHAT THE CALCULATOR HANDLES

Sling math is statics with consequences. A load W on n legs at angle θ from horizontal puts T = W/(n·sinθ) in each leg — at 60° each of two legs carries 58% of the load, at 30° each carries 100%, and below that the tension grows toward infinity as the sling flattens. Hitch style scales capacity: a choker grips at about 75% of vertical rating, a true basket doubles it. When the center of gravity sits off-center, the leg NEARER the CoG carries more — computed exactly from moment equilibrium. This module refuses to print a number below 30°, because that is what a qualified rigger would do.

THE MATH BEHIND IT

  • T = W / (n_eff · sin θ) (θ from HORIZONTAL)
  • Angle factor = 1 / sin θ (1.155 @ 60°, 1.414 @ 45°, 2.0 @ 30°)
  • Hitch: vertical ×1.0 · choker ×0.75 · basket ×2.0
  • V₁ = W·d₂/(d₁+d₂) (vertical share, offset CoG)
  • T₁ = V₁·L₁/h with L = √(d² + h²)
  • Shoulder eyebolt: 100% in-line, ~30% @ 45°, ~25% @ 90°

References & StandardsASME B30.9 (slings), B30.26 (rigging hardware), B30.20 / BTH-1 (below-the-hook devices), OSHA 1926.251

WHEN TO USE THIS MODULE

Plan any lift where sling angle, hitch, or an offset CoG is in play: machine skids, vessels with nozzles all on one end, a gearbox with the motor still bolted on. Compute the per-leg tension, divide by the hitch factor, and buy slings whose Working Load Limit covers the worst leg — both slings, sized for the near-leg tension, so the load cannot be hooked up backwards. Then walk the answer into the hardware: shackles, hooks, and the eyebolt derates that fall off a cliff with angle.

Worked Example: Offset-CoG Skid

A 10 kN skid has its CoG 1 m from lug 1 and 2 m from lug 2; the hook floats 2 m above the lug plane. Find both leg tensions.

Vertical shares by moment balance: V₁ = 10×2/3 = 6.67 kN, V₂ = 3.33 kN — the near leg carries twice the far leg. Leg geometry: L₁ = √(1²+2²) = 2.24 m at 63.4°, L₂ = √(4+4) = 2.83 m at 45°.

Tensions: T₁ = 6.67 × 2.24/2 = 7.45 kN, T₂ = 3.33 × 2.83/2 = 4.71 kN. Verticals sum to 10.00 kN — the statics close. Buy BOTH slings for ≥ 7.45 kN ÷ 9.81 ≈ 0.76 t so a swapped hookup cannot overload anything, and rig the hook plumb over the CoG or the skid will rotate on pick.

Lift Planning Steps

  1. 1Find the weight from drawings, scale ticket, or calculation — never from memory. Add rigging weight for big lifts.
  2. 2Locate the CoG. Symmetric loads are the exception; anything with a motor, gearbox, or full tank is offset. Test-pick an unknown CoG a few centimeters and watch the tilt.
  3. 3Choose sling count and length so every leg sits at 45-60° from horizontal. Longer slings cost headroom but buy angle.
  4. 4Compute the worst leg tension (this module), divide by the hitch factor, and select slings with WLL above it — every leg gets the worst-leg sling.
  5. 5Check every piece of hardware in the chain: shackle WLL, hook latch, lug weld (Welds module), and the eyebolt derate if anything pulls at an angle.
  6. 6Walk the lift: plumb hook over CoG, tag lines on, no one under the load, test pick at 100 mm, hold, inspect, proceed.

Common Pitfalls

  • Measuring the angle from verticalEvery chart and this module use the angle from HORIZONTAL. Swapping conventions turns a comfortable 60° rig into a lethal 30° one on paper that reads fine.
  • Trusting four legs to shareA rigid load on four slings sits on three at most — usually two, diagonal. Standard practice sizes 3- and 4-leg bridles on two legs carrying everything unless a spreader or equalizing hardware guarantees the share.
  • Choking below 120° of wrapThe published 75% choker factor assumes a reasonable choke angle. Choking a wide load bends the sling past its rating at the choke — below ~120° wrap the derate steepens; consult the sling chart.
  • Side-loading hooks and eyeboltsA shoulder eyebolt at 90° keeps a quarter of its rating; a plain (no-shoulder) eyebolt keeps essentially none — it bends and pops. Hooks side-loaded on the tip lose most of theirs. Use swivel hoist rings for anything that rotates into line.
  • Snatching the loadDynamic amplification on a jerked pick easily doubles the static tension. Cranes accelerate smoothly; come-alongs and forklifts do not — plan the power source accordingly.

Engineer FAQ

Why the hard refusal below 30°?
At 30° each leg already carries the full load share (angle factor 2.0), horizontal compression on the load peaks, and a small further flattening sends tension vertical — er, asymptotic. ASME-based sling charts simply stop at 30°; so does this module.
Do sling weights matter?
For shop lifts, rarely. For long bridles on big lifts, sling and block weight add to W and shift the effective CoG — crane engineering practice includes them above roughly the 10 t class.
Choker or basket for a smooth cylinder?
A basket doubles capacity but the load can roll out of it — baskets need the CoG below the sling contact and ideally a double-wrap. A choker grips and controls rotation at 75% capacity. Control beats capacity: choke it, or basket with a double wrap.
What about the lug itself?
The pad-eye is a structural member: check bearing on the pin hole, shear tear-out, and the weld group (Welds module) at the worst leg tension times any applicable design factor — BTH-1 uses 2.0 on yield for lifting devices, higher for shock.
