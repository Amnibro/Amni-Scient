BEAM DEFLECTION & BENDING

SHEAR, MOMENT, SLOPE AND DEFLECTION FOR STANDARD CASES — PLUS REQUIRED I & S FROM A SAG BUDGET

OPEN BEAM CALCULATOR →

WHAT THE CALCULATOR HANDLES

A loaded beam responds with internal shear V(x), bending moment M(x), slope, and deflection. For the standard cases — simply-supported or fixed ends, point or distributed load — closed-form results exist and are exact within Euler-Bernoulli assumptions (plane sections, small deflections, linear elasticity). Bending stress is σ = M·c/I; deflection scales with 1/EI. This module solves the classic cases, draws the shear and moment diagrams, and its DESIGNER card inverts the problem: give it the load, span, and a deflection budget (L/240 floor, L/360 typical, L/800 crane rail) and it hands back the required I and S to shop for a section with.

KEY EQUATIONS

  • δ_max = P·L³ / (48·E·I) (SS, center point)
  • δ_max = 5·w·L⁴ / (384·E·I) (SS, uniform)
  • δ_max = P·L³ / (3·E·I) (cantilever, tip point)
  • M_max = P·L/4 (SS point) · w·L²/8 (SS uniform) · P·L (cantilever)
  • σ = M / S, S_req = M / σ_allow
  • I_req = deflection formula solved for I at δ = L/ratio

WHEN THIS IS THE RIGHT TOOL

Size anything that bends: a machine base rail, a gantry beam, a mezzanine joist, a shaft between bearings (which is just a round beam), a cantilevered bracket. Engineers usually know the load and how much sag is tolerable — the section is the answer, not the input. Deflection governs more designs than strength: a beam at L/360 under full load is usually loafing at a third of its allowable stress. Check both, buy the section that passes both, then hand the numbers to the Sections module to pick a real profile.

Worked Example: Sizing From a Sag Budget

A simply-supported walkway beam spans 4 m and carries a 10 kN point load at midspan. Deflection budget L/360; steel E = 200 GPa, allowable bending 0.6·Sy = 150 MPa for A36. Find the required section.

Allowable sag: δ = 4000/360 = 11.1 mm. Invert the deflection formula: I_req = P·L³/(48·E·δ) = 10,000 × 4³ / (48 × 200×10⁹ × 0.0111) = 6.0×10⁻⁶ m⁴ = 600 cm⁴. Strength: M = P·L/4 = 10 kN·m, so S_req = M/σ = 10×10⁶/150 = 66,700 mm³ = 66.7 cm³.

An IPE 160 (I = 869 cm⁴, S = 109 cm³) passes both with margin; an IPE 140 (I = 541 cm⁴) fails deflection despite passing strength — which is the usual story. Deflection governed, as it does for most spans past a few meters.

Beam Sizing Procedure

  1. 1Fix the support condition honestly — a bolted end plate is closer to pinned than fixed unless it was designed for moment transfer. When unsure, size as pinned (conservative for midspan moment and deflection).
  2. 2Choose the deflection budget by consequence: L/240 for roofs nobody walks on, L/360 where finishes crack, L/500-L/800 for cranes, rails, and machine alignment.
  3. 3Compute I_req from the deflection formula and S_req from M/σ_allow. The larger demand governs the section pick.
  4. 4Shop a standard profile with both I and S above the requirement — the Sections module holds the common shapes; rolled-section tables have the rest.
  5. 5Check shear only for short stubby spans (L/d < ~8) — bending governs long before shear on ordinary proportions.
  6. 6If the beam also sees axial compression, stop and run the Columns module: beam-columns interact and neither check alone is sufficient.

Common Pitfalls

  • Assuming fixed ends you do not haveA fixed-end beam deflects 1/4 as much as pinned — assuming fixity you cannot guarantee under-sizes the beam by that same factor. End rotation at a "rigid" bolted connection is real; codes treat most connections as pinned unless detailed for moment.
  • Using the wrong axis ISection tables list Ix and Iy. A beam bent about its weak axis can have 5-20× less stiffness. Verify the load direction against the section orientation before lifting I from a table.
  • Forgetting load combinationsDead + live + the forklift that parks there once a week. Deflection checks typically use service (unfactored) loads; strength checks use factored loads. Mixing the two conventions is a silent factor-of-1.5 error.
  • Point-load formulas on distributed realityEquipment feet, pallets and pipe racks spread load. Treating a distributed load as a single center point overestimates moment by up to 2× — sometimes fine (conservative), sometimes it buys a beam two sizes too big.

Engineer FAQ

Why does deflection govern so often?
Because allowable stress grew with better steels but stiffness did not — E is the same 200 GPa in every carbon steel grade. Sections sized to modern strength allowables are slender, so serviceability (sag, vibration, finish cracking) takes over as the binding constraint.
When do I need to worry about lateral-torsional buckling?
When the compression flange is unbraced over a long length — a deep narrow section bent about its strong axis can twist and buckle sideways below the plastic moment. Rules of thumb fail here; use the AISC F2 provisions with the actual unbraced length.
Can I superpose load cases?
Yes — linear elasticity means shear, moment, and deflection from separate loads add algebraically. Solve each standard case, sum the results at matching positions. That is how every beam table is meant to be used.
How do I handle a beam on three supports?
It is statically indeterminate — standard-case formulas no longer apply directly. Use the three-moment theorem, superposition with a redundant reaction, or accept the approximation of two independent spans (conservative for midspan moment, wrong for the middle-support reaction).
