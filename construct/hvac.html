Ductwork is the quiet half of every HVAC bill. Contractors price duct replacement at $25–$55 per installed foot — $2,500–$6,000 for a typical house — while the sheet metal and flex behind that number costs a fraction: 6 in snap-lock pipe at $2–$3 per foot, R-8 insulated flex around $50–$80 per 25 ft bag, registers $10–$25 each. And bad duct design wastes money forever: an undersized return or a crushed flex run can strangle a brand-new system into short-cycling, which is why the sizing math matters more than the parts.

What Moves the Number

  • Tonnage and total airflowCooling needs roughly 400 CFM of air movement per ton, so a 3-ton system has to breathe 1,200 CFM through whatever ducts you give it. Tonnage itself should come from a Manual J load calculation — the square-feet-per-ton folklore swings from 500 to 1,500 depending on climate and insulation, and oversizing is the single most common residential HVAC mistake.
  • Duct materialGalvanized snap-lock trunk and branch runs $2–$4 per foot bare, plus wrap; R-8 insulated flex is $2–$3.50 per foot and installs fast. Metal flows better and lasts; flex performs fine when pulled tight, supported every 4 ft and kept short. Energy code (IECC) wants R-8 insulation on ducts in unconditioned attics, R-6 elsewhere outside the envelope.
  • Registers and returnsPlan about one supply register per 100–150 sq ft, with room CFM set by load, not habit. Returns are where systems actually starve — total return area should match supply, and a single central return needs jumper ducts or door undercuts to pull from closed bedrooms. The balance check flags a starved return before you build it.
  • The static pressure budgetResidential blowers are typically rated around 0.5 in wc of external static, and everything spends it: filters, coils, every elbow, every foot of flex. Manual D sizing at a 0.1 in per 100 ft friction rate keeps the spend sane — add a restrictive MERV 13 filter and long saggy flex and the budget is gone.

What a Typical Project Costs

A 3-ton, 1,200 CFM single-story system for about 1,600 sq ft: air handler in the utility closet, eight supply runs, two returns.

Supply side: plenum $80–$150; eight takeoffs at about $10 — $80; eight boots at $12 — $96; eight registers at $15 — $120; roughly 120 ft of duct between 6 and 8 in — $300–$420 in R-8 flex, somewhat more in wrapped metal. Return side: two filter-ready grilles $60–$80 and about 30 ft of larger return duct $120–$160. Mastic, foil tape, straps and sealant $60.

Duct materials total: about $950–$1,200, against $3,500–$6,000 for the same ductwork installed. Two honest caveats the tool also gives you: the equipment itself (air handler, condenser, lineset) is a separate budget, and anything touching the refrigerant circuit requires EPA 608 certification — hire that out, or sidestep it with a pre-charged DIY mini-split like MRCOOL at $1,500–$3,000 per zone.

Mistakes That Cost Real Money

  • Sizing equipment by square footageAn oversized air conditioner cools the thermostat fast and shuts off before it dehumidifies, forever — clammy rooms and short-cycle wear. Manual J exists because envelope quality moves the load 3× between houses of identical size. Size the load, then the airflow, then the ducts.
  • Crushed and sagging flex ductA 6 in flex run yanked around a tight corner or pinched by a truss flows like 4 in pipe. Support flex every 4 ft with wide straps, keep sag under about 1/2 in per foot, pull the inner liner tight, and treat every extra foot of length as lost airflow.
  • Sealing with cloth duct tapeThe one thing duct tape is provably bad at is ducts — the adhesive bakes out in a season. Typical homes leak 20–30% of conditioned air; seal every joint with mastic or UL 181 foil tape. Many jurisdictions now require a duct-leakage test on new systems, so this is a code item, not a nicety.
  • One central return with the doors closedBedrooms with supplies but no return path pressurize when doors close, forcing air out through the walls while the return starves. Jumper ducts, transfer grilles, or a 1 in door undercut per bedroom fix it cheaply at rough-in and expensively after paint.
  • Spending the static budget on a filterSwapping a builder-grade filter for a thick high-MERV one without upsizing the return can push total external static past the blower rating — airflow drops, coils ice, the new furnace runs worse than the old one. If you want MERV 13, size the return grille area for it; the balance check will show the strain.

FAQ

Can I install my own ductwork?
In many jurisdictions, yes — duct fabrication and installation is homeowner-permittable work under a mechanical permit with inspection, and it is genuinely DIY-friendly sheet-metal-and-flex labor. The refrigerant circuit is the exception: charging and brazing linesets requires EPA 608 certification. Plan and duct it yourself; hire the refrigerant work or choose pre-charged equipment.
What is the difference between Manual J and Manual D?
Manual J is the room-by-room heating and cooling load calculation; Manual S selects equipment to match it; Manual D sizes the ducts to deliver each room's airflow within the blower's static pressure budget. This tool applies Manual D friction-rate sizing to the layout you draw — you bring the loads, it sizes the runs.
Is flex duct bad?
No — badly installed flex is bad. Pulled tight, supported every 4 ft, and kept to short branch runs, R-8 flex performs close to spec at half the installed effort of metal. Long saggy runs and pinched bends are what give it the reputation. The estimator prices either; the table above shows the honest penalty.
How many CFM does each room need?
It falls out of the room's share of the load: a typical bedroom wants 75–125 CFM, a large living area 200–400. Divide the system total (400 CFM per ton) across rooms by load share, set each register's CFM in the sketch, and the duct sizes follow automatically.
