A poured-concrete patio is the cheapest permanent outdoor floor you can put on the ground. Contractor-built, a plain broom-finish slab runs $8–$15 per square foot in most US markets — call it $1,700–$2,900 for a modest 14×12 — and stamped or exposed-aggregate work pushes $12–$22. Pour it yourself and the same slab costs $2.50–$5 per square foot in materials: concrete, base, reinforcement, forms and sealer. Pavers land higher ($4–$8 DIY, $15–$30 installed), loose gravel lower, but concrete is the workhorse: one very hard Saturday, then decades of nothing.

What sinks first-time pours is rarely the finishing — it is the takeoff. Concrete is ordered in cubic yards and arrives on the truck's schedule: run half a yard short and you get a cold joint; order a yard over and you paid to dump it. The free Amni-Construct patio estimator does that math for you. Set the footprint — a rectangle, any drawn polygon or L-shape, or an outline traced to scale from a satellite view of your yard — pick slab thickness, gravel base and reinforcement, and it returns exact concrete volume priced both ways (bags versus ready-mix), a materials list matched against Home Depot and Lowe's, control-joint layout to ACI guidance, and printable layout, section, formwork and joint plans. The Cost Levers

  • Slab thickness and what sits on itFour inches is standard for foot traffic; anything a vehicle parks on wants 5–6 in — one checkbox in the estimator. Every added inch is real money: a 200 sq ft patio needs 2.5 cu yd at 4 in and 3.7 cu yd at 6 in.
  • Bags versus ready-mixAn 80 lb bag costs $5.50–$7 and yields 0.6 cu ft — 45 bags and $250–$315 per cubic yard, plus mixer hours. Ready-mix runs $130–$170 per cu yd delivered, but plants add a $75–$150 short-load fee under roughly 4 cu yd. The crossover sits near 1.5–2 cu yd; the estimator prices both routes side by side.
  • Base and subgradeStandard practice is 4 in of compacted crushed stone, 6 in over clay or in deep-frost states — roughly $40–$60 per ton bulk, and a 14×12 patio at 4 in eats about 3 tons. Pouring on unprepared topsoil is how patios crack and settle in year two.
  • Reinforcement choice6×6 welded wire mesh adds $0.30–$0.50 per sq ft; a #3 rebar grid at 18 in roughly doubles that but handles vehicle loads better; fiber-reinforced mix adds $10–$15 per cu yd at the plant and skips the steel entirely. The estimator counts sheets or bars for whichever you pick.
  • Finish and edge upgradesA broom finish costs nothing but technique. Exposed aggregate and stamping are usually contractor work. A paver border course dresses up a plain slab for $8–$15 per linear foot, and a thickened turndown edge — one checkbox in the tool — buys frost resistance at the perimeter for a few extra cubic feet of concrete.

Worked Example

Run the tool's default: a 14×12 ft patio against the back of the house, 4 in slab on 4 in of gravel, wire mesh, broom finish. That is 168 sq ft and 56 cu ft of concrete — 2.07 cu yd; order 2.5 with waste and subgrade margin.

Ready-mix: 2.5 cu yd at $150 plus a short-load fee — $450–$525. The same pour in 80 lb bags is roughly 103 bags at $6, $620, plus a rented mixer; the truck wins here. Gravel base: about 3 tons of crushed stone — $120–$180 delivered. Mesh: seven 42×84 in sheets — $75. Forms and stakes for the three exposed edges — $70. Expansion foam at the house, poly sheeting, cure-and-seal — $75.

Total materials: about $800–$950 at mid-2026 big-box and plant pricing, versus a typical $1,700–$2,900 contractor quote for the identical slab. Every line lands with a store link, and the formwork and joint sheets print ready for pour day.

How to Approach the Job

  1. 1Check the rules first: grade-level patios are permit-exempt in many jurisdictions, but setback and impervious-cover limits still apply. The estimator's permit panel gives state-level guidance; call 811 before any digging.
  2. 2Set the footprint in the sidebar, or open Draw Shape for an L-shape or any polygon, or use Photo Trace with an address satellite view or an uploaded photo — set scale with two known points and click the corners.
  3. 3Pick slab thickness, gravel base depth and reinforcement. Check the vehicle box if a car will ever sit on it, and the turndown-edge box in frost country — the section drawing updates to match.
  4. 4Choose a finish swatch, and consider the 2 in PVC sleeve option — cheap pipe under the slab today saves cutting concrete for wiring or irrigation later.
  5. 5Open Materials & Pricing: bags versus ready-mix totals, every item with store links. Paste live Home Depot or Lowe's prices, edit any cell, export CSV.
  6. 6Print the 2D plans — layout, section, formwork and joint sheets. The joint plan follows ACI spacing guidance (24–36 times slab thickness: 8–12 ft panels on a 4 in slab).
  7. 7Pour day: square the forms with the 3-4-5 method, set mesh on chairs at mid-slab, screed, bull-float, broom, cut or tool the joints on schedule, and damp-cure for a week.

What Goes Wrong (and How to Avoid It)

  • Ordering by area instead of volumeSubgrades are never perfectly flat — a low spot an inch deep across a corner eats a quarter yard. Order 10% over the geometric volume. Running short mid-pour means a visible, structurally weak cold joint.
  • Skipping base compactionDumping 4 in of gravel and pouring on it is barely better than pouring on dirt. Compact in 2 in lifts with a plate compactor (about $90 a day to rent). Settlement under a slab cannot be fixed — only mudjacked or replaced.
  • Leaving the mesh on the groundWire mesh only controls cracking if it sits in the middle of the slab thickness. Set it on chairs or pull it up as you pour; mesh trodden into the subgrade is money buried in the dirt.
  • No control joints, or cutting them lateConcrete will crack; joints decide where. Tool them during finishing or saw-cut within 6–12 hours, one quarter of slab depth, at ACI spacing. The estimator's joint plan lays out the exact grid.
  • Trapping water against the houseSlope the slab 1/8–1/4 in per foot away from the foundation and isolate it from the house with expansion material. A back-pitched patio funnels every storm at your basement wall.

Homeowner FAQ

How thick should a concrete patio be?
Four inches over 4 in of compacted gravel for foot traffic; 5–6 in with rebar where a vehicle parks. In frost states, a turndown edge resists heave at the perimeter. The estimator adjusts the whole design when you toggle the vehicle option.
Is it cheaper to use bags or order ready-mix?
Under about 1.5 cu yd (a stoop or short walkway), bags win once short-load fees are counted. Above 2 cu yd, ready-mix is cheaper and far less work — 2.5 cu yd is 103 bags through a mixer. The tool prices both from your exact geometry so the crossover is visible, not guessed.
Do I need a permit to pour a patio?
Often not for an at-grade slab, but many towns regulate setbacks and total impervious cover, and attached covers or footings change the answer. The permit panel summarizes your state; your building department is authoritative.
Can one person pour a patio alone?
Up to about a yard, yes. Beyond that, finishing is a race against the set: 2.5 cu yd wants two or three people screeding and floating in rotation. Plan the crew before the truck is booked, or split the pour into sections at the joint lines the plan already gives you.
