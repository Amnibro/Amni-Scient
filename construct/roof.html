Roofing is priced in squares — 100 sq ft units — and the numbers are simple once you have them: architectural shingles run $110–$140 per square in materials at mid-2026 big-box prices, so a typical 17-square gable roof is roughly $2,000 in shingles and $3,000–$3,300 with underlayment, ice barrier, edge metal, ridge cap and ventilation. A contractor installs the same roof for $7,500–$12,000. That gap is real, but so is the honesty required here: a single-story 4/12 or 6/12 roof is DIY territory with fall protection; a steep or two-story roof is not, and this estimator will tell you so rather than pretend otherwise. Falls are the leading killer in construction, and no materials savings prices in a fall.

Even if a crew does the install, doing your own takeoff changes the conversation: you can sanity-check every bid line against your own numbers. The free Amni-Roof estimator measures the roof without you leaving the ground — enter the building footprint (or trace it from a satellite view by address in Photo Trace), set the pitch, and the slope factor converts footprint to true roof area. Pick gable or hip and architectural, 3-tab, metal panel or synthetic, and it returns squares, bundles or panels with waste, underlayment and ice & water rolls, drip edge, starter, ridge and hip cap, ridge/hip/eave lengths, ventilation, a printable RF-1 roof plan, and store-priced totals — all in the browser, nothing installed.

What Drives the Cost

  • PitchPitch is the multiplier on everything. A 6/12 roof has 1.118× its footprint in actual surface; a 12/12 has 1.414× — 41% more shingles, underlayment and nails for the same house. It also sets the labor class: past about 7/12 crews charge steep-slope rates and DIY stops being reasonable.
  • Gable vs hipA hip roof trades rake edges for four planes, hip caps, and diagonal cuts along every hip line — plan 15% waste against 10% for a plain gable. The estimator recomputes cap lengths and waste when you flip the type, and the 3D view shows exactly where the extra cutting happens.
  • MaterialArchitectural shingles $110–$140 per square; 3-tab $90–$110 and disappearing from shelves; exposed-fastener metal panels $200–$400 per square in materials; standing seam $400–$700; synthetic slate and shake $400+. Metal roughly doubles or triples the material bill and doubles the service life.
  • Underlayment and ice barrierSynthetic underlayment runs about $50 per 10-square roll and has effectively replaced felt. In cold climates IRC R905.1.2 requires a self-adhering ice barrier from the eave to at least 24 in inside the exterior wall line — about $65 per 2-square roll — plus valleys and penetrations.
  • Tear-off vs overlayA tear-off adds a $400–$600 dumpster and a day of hard labor but exposes the deck — the only chance to fix rot for the next 25 years. Some jurisdictions allow one overlay; it hides deck damage, adds weight, telegraphs old curling through the new layer, and usually shortens the new roof's life.
  • Edges, flashing and ventilationDrip edge is code at eaves and rakes (IRC R905.2.8.5) at roughly $10 per 10-ft stick; add pipe boots, step flashing at sidewalls, and a balanced vent system — ridge vent (~$11 per 4-ft section) matched to soffit intake at the 1:150 or 1:300 rule. Skipped ventilation cooks shingles from below and voids warranties.

What a Typical Project Costs

Take the estimator's default building: 40×30 ft footprint, 6/12 gable, 12-in overhangs, architectural shingles. With overhangs the roof covers 42×32 ft = 1,344 sq ft of footprint; the 6/12 slope factor of 1.118 makes that 1,503 sq ft of actual roof, and 10% gable waste brings the order to 16.5 squares — 50 bundles at about $42: $2,100.

The rest of the system: two 10-square rolls of synthetic underlayment $110, two rolls of ice & water for the 84 ft of eaves $130, sixteen sticks of drip edge for 156 lineal feet of edge $160, starter strip $110, two bundles of ridge cap for the 42-ft ridge $120, ridge vent $110, and nails, pipe boots and step flashing $175.

Total materials: about $3,000–$3,200 at mid-2026 pricing, before a tear-off dumpster. Installed quotes for this roof run $450–$700 per square — $7,400–$11,600 — and every line of that bid is now something you can check against your own takeoff.

Mistakes That Cost Real Money

  • Guessing the pitch from the groundEyeballed pitch is reliably wrong by a step or two, and one step is 5–8% of area — a full square on an average roof. Measure at a rafter or gable end. The estimator's slope factor is exact once the pitch is real.
  • Skipping starter stripThe first course at eaves and rakes needs a starter with the sealant band at the edge — it is what glues the field down against wind. Improvised cut-up 3-tabs put the sealant in the wrong place, and the first storm finds every eave shingle that is not bonded.
  • Omitting the ice barrier in snow countryIce dams push meltwater uphill, under shingles that shed only downhill water. IRC R905.1.2 requires the membrane from eave to 24 in inside the warm wall for a reason — the repair for a rotted eave deck costs more than the whole membrane order.
  • Working a roof you cannot walkAbove 6/12, above one story, or on morning dew, the job changes category. Rent anchors and a harness as the minimum, and treat steep or high roofs as contractor work — the estimator flags steep pitches in its warnings panel, and that warning is the cheapest safety equipment you will ever get.

FAQ

How many bundles of shingles per square?
Three bundles per square for nearly all architectural shingles (33.3 sq ft per bundle); a few thick premium lines take four. The wrapper states coverage per bundle — enter the real product and the estimator counts bundles, not just squares.
Can I measure my roof without climbing on it?
Yes — that is the whole design of the tool. Footprint times slope factor equals roof area, and the footprint comes from a tape measure at ground level or a traced satellite image in Photo Trace. Add the overhang setting, since eaves and rakes extend the roof past the walls by a foot or more on each side.
Is a metal roof worth the extra cost?
Materials run 2–3× architectural shingles, but service life is 40–70 years against 22–30, and insurance discounts apply in hail and wildfire regions. Exposed-fastener panels are the budget entry — just budget a screw-gasket re-torque around year 15–20. Standing seam eliminates that but roughly doubles the panel cost.
Do I need a permit to reroof my house?
For a full replacement, almost every US jurisdiction says yes, and many inspect the deck or the ice barrier before covering. Overlay rules vary widely — some areas allow a second layer, none allow a third. The estimator's Code & Permits panel gives state and city guidance; your building department is the final word.
