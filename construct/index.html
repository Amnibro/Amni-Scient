Field guide

Site know-how that saves a return trip.

📞 Call 811 before you dig — it's free and it's the law
Two to three business days before ANY digging — even one fence post — call 811 or use your state's online portal. Utilities come mark gas, electric, water, and fiber lines with paint and flags for free. Hit an unmarked line without a locate ticket and you're liable for the repair (gas line strikes start around $5,000); with a ticket, you're usually covered. The marks are only valid for about 2 weeks — re-call if your project slips.
❄️ Frost depth — why footings go so deep
Wet soil expands ~9% when it freezes and can lift a structure with tons of force (frost heave). Anything attached to your house must bear below the local frost line so it can't move independently: 6–12" deep in the South, 36–48" in the northern U.S. Your building department publishes the exact number. Free-floating patios and freestanding decks can ride the frost — that's why they're allowed on shallow bases — but never bolt a frost-floating structure to a frost-protected one.
📍 Property pins — find your REAL lot line
Your fence is not your lot line. Surveyors set iron pins or rebar at every corner, usually 0–12" below grade. Pull your plat map (county GIS site, free), measure from a known corner, and sweep with a cheap metal detector or borrow one. Building even a few inches over the line can legally become your neighbor's structure — or force a teardown. If pins are gone, a boundary survey runs $400–800 and is the cheapest insurance you'll ever buy.
🚧 Setbacks & easements — the invisible no-build zones
Setback: the minimum distance from your lot line where structures may sit — commonly 5–10 ft sides, 10–25 ft rear, more in front. Easement: a strip of YOUR land that utilities or drainage have rights to — build a patio on one and the utility may legally demolish it to reach their line. Both are on your plat map and in the zoning code. Impervious-coverage caps (max % of lot you can pave/roof) are the third silent killer — concrete patios count.
🧱 Concrete doesn't dry — it cures
Concrete hardens by a water-consuming chemical reaction (hydration), not by drying. Keep it damp for 7 days — mist it, or cover with plastic/curing blankets — and it gets stronger; let it dry out early and it stays weak forever. It hits ~70% strength at 7 days, rated strength at 28 days. Foot traffic after 24–48 h, cars after 7 days, sealer after 28. And never have extra water added to the truck to make it pour easier — every added gallon costs strength.
🪵 Pressure-treated lumber — ratings & the corrosion trap
PT lumber comes in Use Category ratings: UC3B for above-ground (deck boards, rails), UC4A "Ground Contact" for posts, joists near grade, anything hard to replace. The copper preservative eats ordinary steel — use hot-dip galvanized (G185/ZMAX) or stainless hangers, bolts, and screws, never bright or electroplated fasteners. New PT is soaked wet: let it dry 4–8 weeks (water no longer beads) before stain or paint will stick.
💧 Grading & drainage — water always wins
Ground should fall 6" in the first 10 ft away from your foundation; patios and walks slope 1/8–1/4" per foot away from the house. Downspouts need 4–6 ft extensions minimum. A soggy low spot wants a French drain: trench wrapped in landscape fabric, 4" perforated pipe on gravel, sloped 1% to daylight. Never pour a slab that dams water against the house — you're building a swimming pool for your basement.

📐 Square corners without math — 3-4-5 & diagonals
Measure 3 ft along one side, 4 ft along the other; the diagonal between the marks is exactly 5 ft when the corner is square (any multiple works — 6-8-10 is more accurate). For a whole rectangle, measure both diagonals: equal diagonals = square layout. Set up batter boards (stakes + horizontal cross-pieces) a few feet outside each corner and stretch mason line between them — you can dig and pour without disturbing your layout, and the line reattaches in the same spot every time.

🌡️ Temperature windows — when you can pour, stain & plant
Concrete: pour between 40–90°F and never on frozen ground; below 40°F use blankets, above 90°F pour at dawn and keep it wet. Stain/sealer/paint: 50–90°F, dry wood, out of direct midday sun, no rain for 24 h, and watch the OVERNIGHT low not the daytime high. Polymeric sand: needs 32°F+ and a dry forecast. Sod & trees: plant in spring or fall — summer transplants spend all their energy not dying.
⚓ Posts never touch dirt — footings & standoff bases
Wood in soil rots from the bottom up, even "ground contact" PT. The durable stack is: concrete footing below frost depth → 1" standoff post base (the metal bracket that holds the post off the concrete) → post. Cardboard tube forms make clean footings; bell-bottom shapes resist frost grab in cold climates. A post sleeved in concrete ("post in a bucket") traps water against the wood — it's the slowest way to build a future repair.
🛠️ The hardscape tool list — rent, don't buy
Own (cheap, reused forever): 4-ft level, mason line + line level, torpedo level, framing square, flat shovel, steel rake, hand tamper, 25-ft tape, marking paint, knee pads. Rent by the day: plate compactor (~$90 — non-negotiable for bases), demo hammer, sod cutter, power auger for post holes, concrete mixer. Concrete day only: magnesium float, edger, groover, steel trowel, bull float — buy the cheap set once; they last decades.
🔌 Burying wire & pipe — depth rules + the sleeve trick
NEC burial depths for outdoor circuits: UF-B cable direct-buried 24", PVC conduit 18", rigid metal 6"; under a driveway everything is 24". Low-voltage landscape wire only needs 6". The trick everyone wishes they knew: before ANY slab or walkway pour, lay a couple of 2–4" PVC sleeves under it, capped, ends marked. Ten dollars now means future wire, irrigation, or gas line slides under — no saw-cutting, no jackhammer, no tunnel digging.
🌳 Mulch volcanoes kill trees — and roots crack slabs
Mulch piled against a trunk holds moisture on bark, invites rot and girdling roots, and slowly kills the tree — 2–3" deep, donut not volcano, pulled 3–6" back from the trunk. Going the other way: tree roots extend 2–3× the canopy width and WILL find your slab edge. Keep large trees 10+ ft from patios (small ornamentals 5 ft), or accept lifted, cracked concrete in a decade. Root barriers help but aren't magic.
💦 Outdoor faucets, hoses & the freeze that floods walls
A garden hose left connected in winter traps water in the faucet, freezes, and splits the pipe inside your wall — the flood shows up next spring. Every fall: disconnect hoses, drain them, shut interior shutoffs if you have them. Better: install frost-free sillcocks (the valve seat sits inside the heated wall — but they ONLY work with the hose off). Add a vacuum breaker ($5, screws on) so fertilizer or pool water can't siphon back into your drinking water.
🏠 Ten-minute house savers most people skip
Gutters: clean twice a year — overflow rots fascia and floods basements. GFCI: every outdoor outlet needs one; test the buttons yearly. Caulk: walk the exterior each fall, recaulk gaps at trim, penetrations, and tub surrounds — water entry is the #1 repair multiplier. Water heater: drain a bucket of sediment yearly. Furnace filter: every 1–3 months. Dryer vent: brush the duct yearly (2,900 house fires/yr). Main shutoff: find it BEFORE the pipe bursts, tag it, and turn it once a year so it doesn't seize.
