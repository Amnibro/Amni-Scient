The sticker shock in raised-bed gardening is never the wood — it is the dirt. A 4×8 bed filled 10 inches deep holds almost exactly one cubic yard of soil; in $9 bags of branded raised-bed mix that yard costs $145–$180, while a bulk 50/50 topsoil-compost blend from a landscape yard is $35–$60 plus delivery. Three beds is the difference between roughly $450 bagged and $250 delivered. Frames span a similar spread: a cedar 4×8 kit retails for $180–$350, the same bed built from cedar 2×6 stock is $130–$170, ground-contact pressure-treated $70–$90.

Set your location and the Build Guide becomes a season-by-season schedule keyed to your frost dates, and the GD-1 layout and GD-2 detail sheets print for the fridge door. The Cost Levers

  • Bed material and heightCedar lasts 10–15 years in soil contact at about double the cost of ground-contact pressure-treated, which modern copper-azole chemistry makes acceptable for edibles per most university extension guidance. Galvanized steel kits run $60–$200 and outlast both. Height is a soil decision as much as a comfort one: 10 in to 17 in on a 4×8 bed takes the fill from 1 to 1.7 cubic yards; the planner rescales when depth changes.
  • Soil sourcing: bags versus bulkBagged mixes are $8–$10 per 1.5 cu ft — convenient below about a cubic yard, painful above it. Bulk blends (roughly half topsoil, 30–40% compost, the rest aeration material) run $35–$60 per cubic yard plus $50–$100 delivery. The tool sums every bed into one yardage figure, so the bulk order is a single phone call.
  • Plant spacing drives the countTomatoes want 18–24 in, peppers 18 in, lettuce 6–8 in, carrots 2–3 in. Spacing decides what a bed can actually produce: a 4×8 is 8 tomato plants or about 72 heads of lettuce, not both. The planner draws rows and counts per bed — the nursery trip becomes a list, not a guess.
  • IrrigationA half-inch drip mainline with quarter-inch emitter lines costs $60–$140 for a three-to-four-bed garden, plus $30–$60 for a hose-bib timer. Against hand-watering, drip pays for itself in one vacation. The materials list includes the tubing, emitters and fittings for the beds you drew.

A Real-Numbers Example

A sensible starter garden: two 4×8 beds and one 4×4, filled 10 in deep. Soil volume is 26.7 + 26.7 + 13.3 = 66.7 cu ft — just under 2.5 cubic yards; order 3 for settling. Bulk 50/50 blend at $45 with $75 delivery: $210. The same fill bagged is about 45 large bags — $405 and a lot of lifting.

Frames in cedar: six 2×6×8 boards in two courses plus corner posts and exterior screws per 4×8 bed — about $150 each, $105 for the 4×4, so $405 total; the same three beds in ground-contact pressure-treated run about $230. Add a drip kit with timer — $130; a cubic yard of wood-chip path mulch — $40 bulk; hardware-cloth bottoms if you have gophers — $45.

Total: roughly $560 in pressure-treated, $760 in cedar, soil delivered — and the planner's CSV pairs every line with a store link for aisle price-checks. The first season's harvest will not pay that back; the second one usually does.

Where DIYers Go Wrong

  • Buying soil in bags without doing the yardageA single 4×8 bed at 10 in swallows nearly twenty 1.5 cu ft bags. Anything past one bed, take the planner's cubic-yard total to two landscape yards — bulk typically halves the soil budget, and soil is the biggest line.
  • Beds wider than you can reachFour feet is the maximum width you can work from both sides; against a fence, 30 in. Stepping in compacts the soil you paid to keep loose — the whole point of the raised bed disappears.
  • Cheap wood in permanent soil contactUntreated pine frames sag and rot in 2–4 seasons; salvaged railroad ties leach creosote and have no business near food. Cedar, ground-contact-rated pressure-treated or steel are the three materials worth screwing together.
  • Ignoring the sun before sitingFruiting crops need 6–8 hours of direct sun, measured after nearby trees leaf out, not in February. Watch the yard for a day before you commit — rearranging beds in the planner is free; rearranging filled ones is a shovel job.
  • Planting by the calendar instead of the frost dateZone maps are averages; one warm week fools everyone. Key plantings to your local last-frost date and soil temperature — the planner's schedule does the per-crop offsets once your location is set — and hold transplants a week longer than feels patient.

Questions People Actually Ask

How much soil do I need for a 4×8 raised bed?
At 6 in deep, 16 cu ft (about 0.6 cubic yard); at 10 in, 26.7 cu ft (very nearly a full yard); at 17 in, 45 cu ft (1.7 yards). Multiply out each bed, or let the planner total the whole garden into one bulk-order figure.
Is pressure-treated lumber safe for vegetable beds?
Modern PT uses copper azole or ACQ, not the arsenic-based CCA phased out of residential lumber in 2004, and extension-service studies show minimal copper migration into crops. For zero contact anyway, staple 6-mil poly to the inside walls (never the bottom — drainage) or spend up for cedar or steel.
Are raised beds worth it over planting in the ground?
An in-ground row garden is cheaper by hundreds of dollars. Raised beds buy drainage, imported soil over bad clay or contaminated urban dirt, fewer weeds, a back that does not hate you, and soil that warms about two weeks earlier in spring. If your native soil is good and your knees are too, rows are a fine answer.
How many plants fit in my beds?
Pure spacing arithmetic, done per bed by the planner: a 4×8 holds 8 tomatoes, about 10 peppers, roughly 72 lettuce heads, or several hundred carrots. Mixing crops in one bed is fine — assign multiple plants and the layout splits the rows.
Do I need a permit to build raised beds?
Essentially never for the beds themselves. Watch instead for HOA rules, front-yard gardening ordinances, easements, and fence-height limits if you add deer protection. The planner's location panel surfaces state notes; your town clerk settles the rest.
